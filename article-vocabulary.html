Vocabulary

Vocabulary questions point to one word or phrase in the text and ask which option is closest in meaning. The catch is that the question is about the meaning in this text. Many English words have several meanings, and the most familiar one is often the wrong answer.

What the question tests

You need two things: knowledge of the word, and the ability to use the context to choose the right meaning. The context will rarely define the word for you, but it almost always narrows the choice: it tells you whether the meaning is positive or negative, whether the word describes size, quality, time, or a feeling, and what idea it continues or contrasts with.

Typical wording

  • The word "…" in the passage is closest in meaning to
  • The word "…" in paragraph 2 is closest in meaning to
  • In the sentence "…", the word "…" is closest in meaning to
  • In the email, the phrase "…" is closest in meaning to

In academic passages, the tested word is usually academic vocabulary (viable, disparities, prized, feats). In Daily Life texts, it is more often an everyday or informal word or phrase (scenic, exceed, pushed back).

How it looks on the screen

Five kinds of context clues

ClueHow it worksExample
Definition or restatementThe text explains the word right next to it.The charts were crude, with rough outlines and missing islands.
Contrastbut, yet, however, although signal the opposite idea.The charts were crude, yet navigators found them invaluable.
Examplesuch as, for example, including give instances of the word.Perishable goods, such as milk and fresh fish, …
Cause and effectbecause, so, as a result link the word to a reason or result.The bridge was obsolete, so the city replaced it.
ToneThe surrounding sentences are positive or negative.The results were dismal: sales fell for the third year.

False friends Watch out for false friends: English words that look like Russian words but mean something else. Actual = real (not "current"); accurate = correct, exact (not "neat"); sympathetic = showing understanding (not "good-looking"); fabric = cloth (not "factory"); decade = ten years; magazine = a publication (not "a shop").

Strategies for Vocabulary Questions

Step 1: Go to the word and read around it.

Read the whole sentence with the word, plus the sentences before and after. The clue is often in the next sentence.

Step 2: Predict before you look.

Cover the options and think of your own simple synonym. Then look for the option closest to it.

Step 3: Substitute each option.

Put each option into the sentence in place of the word. Wrong answers usually sound strange or change the meaning of the sentence.

Step 4: Check the direction.

Is the meaning positive, negative or neutral? Is it about size, time, quality, or feeling? Eliminate options that go in the wrong direction.

Step 5: Distrust the most familiar meaning.

If an option matches the meaning you know best but does not fit this context, it is probably the trap.

Step 6: Do not overthink.

If two options seem possible, choose the one that fits the whole sentence and the tone of the text best, and move on.

Common Traps

  • The common meaning. The option gives a real meaning of the word, just not the one used in this sentence.
  • Related to the topic, not to the word. The option fits the subject of the passage but is not a synonym of the tested word.
  • Misleading word parts. invaluable is not "not valuable"; priceless is not "cheap."
  • Same grammar, different meaning. Every option will fit the grammar of the sentence, so grammar alone cannot decide.
Example 1: two words in an academic passage

Early Sea Charts

Long before satellites, sailors relied on hand-drawn charts of the coast. Early charts were often crude, with rough outlines and missing islands, yet experienced navigators considered them invaluable: even an imperfect chart could warn a crew about rocks that had sunk other ships.

1. The word "crude" in the passage is closest in meaning to

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

Crude has several meanings: crude oil (unprocessed), a crude joke (rude), a crude drawing (simple, not detailed). The passage itself explains which one is meant: the charts had "rough outlines and missing islands." So here crude = simple and rough.

  • Choice A – "offensive" is the meaning in a crude joke, not the meaning here.
  • Choice C – "oily" comes from crude oil and has nothing to do with charts.
  • Choice D – the charts are early, but "crude" describes their quality, not their age.

2. The word "invaluable" in the passage is closest in meaning to

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

This is a classic trap. The prefix in- usually means "not," so many students choose worthless. But invaluable means "so valuable that you cannot measure its value." The context confirms it: the word follows yet (a contrast with "crude") and the next part explains that the charts could save ships.

  • Choice A – the opposite meaning; yet shows the navigators valued the charts despite their faults.
  • Choice C – "valuable" can mean expensive, but the passage is about how useful the charts were, not their price.
  • Choice D – nothing in the passage suggests the charts were rare or strange.
Example 2: a phrase in a Daily Life text

Social media post

Hi all, quick update: the deadline for the photo contest has been pushed back to May 10, so you have two more weeks to send in your entries. — Jordan, Photo Club

1. In the post, the phrase "pushed back" is closest in meaning to

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

Phrasal verbs and informal phrases are common in Daily Life texts. The next part of the sentence is the key: you have two more weeks. If people get more time, the deadline is later.

  • Choice A – the contest is still happening; there is a new deadline.
  • Choice C – an earlier deadline would give less time, not "two more weeks."
  • Choice D – the deadline changed, so it was not simply confirmed.
