Purpose Questions

A Purpose question points to one particular thing the speaker said (an example, a comment, a story, a short phrase) and asks why they said it. In other words: What is the speaker trying to do with this? Give an example? Refuse an invitation? Support a claim? The answer is about the function of the words, not their content.

What it asksWhy the speaker mentions, describes or says something specific
Where it appearsListen to a Conversation · Listen to an Announcement · Listen to an Academic Talk
Especially common inAcademic talks (Why does the speaker mention an example?) and conversations (Why does the man say, "…"?)
Main skillConnecting a part of the recording to the point it serves

How to Recognize It

  • Why does the speaker mention …?
  • Why does the professor describe …?
  • What is the purpose of the speaker's comment about …?
  • Why does the woman say, "…"?
  • Why does the speaker quote …?
  • Why does the man talk about …?

In the Official Guide examples, the options begin with To + verb: To explain…, To give an example of…, To show that…, To encourage…

Purpose vs. Main Idea vs. Inference

These three types can sound alike. The difference is what they ask about:

TypeQuestionAsks about
Main IdeaWhat is the main purpose of the announcement?The whole recording
PurposeWhy does the speaker mention that another group waited an hour?The function of one part
InferenceWhat does the man mean when he says, "…"?The hidden meaning of one part

"I've had better days": the meaning is it went badly (Inference); the purpose might be to avoid giving details or to show disappointment (Purpose). Meaning answers what, purpose answers why.

Every Detail Serves a Point

In a good talk, nothing is random. Each example, story or number is there to support an idea. The Purpose question asks you to follow the arrow back from the detail to the idea:

Detail (what the question names)

A jacket for $500, then a similar one for $200

serves
Point (the answer)

The first price we see affects how we judge the next one

Correct option: To show how a first price affects our judgment of later prices. Wrong options talk about the jacket itself: its quality, its cost, where to buy it.

The point is usually stated right before or right after the detail: "This is called anchoring", "So anchoring isn't about being careless." That's why notes that put examples under their idea are so useful.

Common Functions

The correct answer names one of these functions. Know the verbs, and match them to what the speaker is really doing.

In academic talks

  • To introduce the topic (the hook)
  • To give an example of a concept
  • To support a claim with evidence
  • To contrast two ideas or cases
  • To explain something with an analogy
  • To correct a common misunderstanding
  • To point out a limitation
  • To emphasize a key point

In conversations and announcements

  • To explain why they can't do something
  • To decline an invitation politely
  • To encourage or reassure someone
  • To express doubt or surprise
  • To suggest another option
  • To show approval or agreement
  • To justify a decision or a change
  • To make a joke about themselves

How Wrong Options Are Built

TrapHow it works
What instead of whyCorrectly describes the detail, but gives it the wrong job. To compare the quality of two jackets. The most common trap.
The neighboring pointConnects the detail to a different idea from the recording, often the one just before or after.
The wrong verbThe right topic with the wrong intention: to criticize instead of to explain, to reject instead of to qualify.
Too strongTo prove, to reject completely when the speaker only illustrates or partly agrees.
Literal readingTakes a joke or an idiom at face value: "I'm drowning in lab reports" → To ask for help with his lab reports.

Strategies for Purpose Questions

  • Find the detail in your notes, then look one line up. Which idea was it attached to? The answer names that idea.
  • Listen for signal phrases. "For example…", "Let me give you…", "Take…", "Now, you might think…", "This brings me to…" announce the function of what follows.
  • In conversations, look at the situation. Was the speaker just invited, criticized, asked for help? Their words are a reaction, and the purpose follows from it.
  • Check the verb of each option. Ask: is the speaker really criticizing? really proving? The verb is where most wrong options fail.
  • Reject options that only describe. If an option just says what the detail is, and not what it does, it's the "what instead of why" trap.
Example

You hear:

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a psychology class.

Suppose you walk into a shop and see a jacket for five hundred dollars. Way too expensive, right? Then, right next to it, you see a similar jacket for two hundred. Suddenly two hundred feels like a bargain, even though, if you'd seen it first, you might have thought it was expensive too. This is called anchoring. The first number we see becomes an anchor, and we judge everything after it in relation to that number. Now, you might think this only works on shoppers who aren't paying attention. But in one well-known study, experienced judges were asked to decide on sentences for the same imaginary case. Before deciding, they rolled a pair of dice. Judges who rolled high numbers gave longer sentences on average. The dice had nothing to do with the case, yet they still made a difference. So anchoring isn't about being careless. It's how all of our brains handle numbers.

1. Why does the speaker mention a jacket for five hundred dollars?

ATo show how a first price affects our judgment of the next one
BTo complain about the high cost of clothing
CTo compare the quality of two jackets
DTo give students advice on saving money

2. Why does the speaker describe the study with judges?

ATo show that judges often make careless decisions
BTo explain how prison sentences are decided
CTo show that even experts are influenced by anchoring
DTo introduce a new method of training judges
Answers + Explanation

Correct Answers: 1 – A, 2 – C

Explanation

Question 1. The jacket story is the hook, and the very next sentences name the point: "This is called anchoring. The first number we see becomes an anchor…" The jacket serves that idea. A.

  • C is what instead of why: there are two jackets, but their quality is never the point.
  • B uses the wrong verb: "way too expensive" is part of the example, not a complaint.
  • D is not supported.

Question 2. Look at the line before the study: "you might think this only works on shoppers who aren't paying attention", and the line after: "So anchoring isn't about being careless." The study with experienced judges corrects that misunderstanding. C.

  • A is the opposite of the point: the talk says anchoring is not about carelessness.
  • B is what instead of why: the study involves sentences, but it's not about how they are decided.
  • D is not mentioned.

Practice: 8 Purpose Questions

Two conversations, an announcement and an academic talk. Each recording plays only once. For every question, find the detail, then ask: what was this helping the speaker to do?

1 of 4 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between two students.

Woman: Have you thought about running for president of the photography club?

Man: Me? I can barely keep track of my own schedule.

Woman: Come on, you organized that whole exhibition last spring. Everyone loved it.

Man: That was different. I had three months and a lot of help.

Woman: You'd have help this time too. Jonah and I already said we'd be on the committee.

Man: Well, if you two are in, I guess I could think about it.

1. Why does the man say, "I can barely keep track of my own schedule"?

ATo ask the woman for help with his schedule
BTo show that he doubts he could handle the role
CTo complain about the club's meeting times
DTo suggest that the woman should run instead
Show answer

B. It is his reaction to the idea of becoming president: he jokes that he can't even manage his own time, so how could he lead a club? B. A is the literal-reading trap. C and D are not suggested.

2. Why does the woman mention that she and Jonah will be on the committee?

ATo show that they are more qualified than the man
BTo explain why last spring's exhibition was successful
CTo invite the man to join the committee
DTo reassure the man that he would not be working alone
Show answer

D. The man says the exhibition worked because he had a lot of help. She answers "You'd have help this time too" and names the helpers. Her purpose is to remove his worry: D. It works, since he agrees to think about it. B is the neighboring point (the exhibition). C reverses the roles: he would be president, not a committee member.

2 of 4 · Announcement

Narrator: Listen to an announcement on a class trip.

Good morning, class. Before we head into the museum, a quick word. The special exhibition on the second floor is extremely popular, and last week one of our groups had to wait almost an hour to get in. So we'll start there, right when the doors open, and see the permanent collection afterward. Please stay together until we're through the entrance. Once we're inside, you're free to explore on your own until noon.

Why does the speaker mention that another group waited almost an hour?

ATo complain about the museum's staff
BTo warn students that they may miss lunch
CTo explain why the class will visit the special exhibition first
DTo show how popular the permanent collection is
Show answer

C. Right after the story comes "So we'll start there, right when the doors open". The long wait justifies the order of the visit: C. D is the neighboring-point trap: the popular one is the special exhibition, not the permanent collection. A uses the wrong verb: she isn't complaining. B is not mentioned.

3 of 4 · Academic talk

Narrator: Listen to a talk in an art history class.

Why did the Impressionists paint outdoors so much more than earlier artists? Part of the answer is an invention that seems very small: the metal paint tube. Before the eighteen forties, painters bought pigments as powder and mixed them with oil themselves. The mixture didn't last long, so it was stored in pig bladders, which were hard to carry and often burst. Honestly, it's no surprise most painting happened in the studio. Then an American painter living in London, John Rand, patented a squeezable tin tube. Suddenly, paint could be carried anywhere, kept for weeks, and used straight away. Renoir reportedly said that without paint in tubes, there would have been no Impressionism. That may be a bit of an exaggeration, but the tubes clearly changed how artists worked. Painting outdoors meant working fast, before the light changed, which helps explain the quick, visible brushstrokes that Impressionism is known for.

1. Why does the speaker mention pig bladders?

ATo show how difficult it was to carry paint before tubes
BTo describe an unusual subject of early paintings
CTo explain where painters got their pigments
DTo show how creative painters had to be
Show answer

A. Bladders were hard to carry and often burst, and the speaker concludes that it's no surprise painting stayed in the studio. The detail shows the problem that tubes later solved: A. C is what instead of why: pigments came as powder, and bladders were only for storage. B and D are not supported.

2. Why does the speaker quote Renoir?

ATo prove that Renoir invented Impressionism
BTo criticize Renoir for exaggerating
CTo show how an artist of the time saw the importance of tubes
DTo introduce a new topic
Show answer

C. The quote supports the talk's point that tubes mattered, using the words of an Impressionist himself. C. A is too strong and not said. B uses the wrong verb: she calls it an exaggeration but still agrees with the idea. D: the topic stays the same.

3. Why does the speaker say, "That may be a bit of an exaggeration"?

ATo reject Renoir's opinion completely
BTo suggest that Renoir was not an Impressionist
CTo suggest that tubes were invented later
DTo accept the main idea but limit how strongly it is stated
Show answer

D. She continues "but the tubes clearly changed how artists worked". She agrees with the idea, just not with its strongest form. D. A is too strong: "a bit of" and the "but" show only partial disagreement. B and C are not supported.

4 of 4 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between a student and a professor.

Man: Professor Okafor, do you have a minute? It's about my research paper.

Woman: Of course. What's on your mind?

Man: I was planning to write about renewable energy, but every source I find is about solar panels.

Woman: Well, that tells you something, doesn't it? The topic is enormous. What if you focused on just one country, or one type of energy?

Man: Hmm. My family's from Iceland, and they use a lot of geothermal energy there.

Woman: Now that's a paper I'd like to read.

1. Why does the professor say, "That tells you something, doesn't it?"

ATo point out that the man's topic is too broad
BTo ask where the man found his sources
CTo criticize the man's research skills
DTo suggest that he write about solar panels
Show answer

A. She follows it immediately with "The topic is enormous" and suggests narrowing it. Her remark leads him to see the problem himself: A. C is the wrong verb: she is guiding, not criticizing. D misuses the solar panel detail. B is not what she means.

2. Why does the professor say, "Now that's a paper I'd like to read"?

ATo ask to read the man's draft right away
BTo show approval of the man's new idea
CTo express doubt about the new topic
DTo change the subject of the conversation
Show answer

B. The man has just narrowed his topic to geothermal energy in Iceland, exactly what she suggested. Her comment is enthusiastic approval: B. A is the literal-reading trap: there is no draft yet. C is the opposite of her tone.
