Listen to an Academic Talk

In this task, you hear a short lecture: a teacher explaining one idea from history, biology, psychology, business, art or another subject. It sounds like part of a class or an educational podcast. After the talk, you answer four questions.

This is the longest recording in the Listening section and the one with the most questions. It checks whether you can follow an explanation: what the main idea is, how the speaker supports it, why they give certain examples and how the talk is organized. You don't need any knowledge of the subject: every term you need is explained in the talk.

What you hearOne speaker, usually a professor, giving a short talk on an academic topic
What you seeA picture of the speaker. After the audio, the questions appear one at a time.
Length of the audio175–250 words, about 1.5–2 minutes
Questions per talk4
Scored questions4 in Module 1 (one talk); none in Module 2 Lower; 8 in Module 2 Upper (two talks)
Where in the sectionAt the end of each module
LevelA2–C2 (the widest range in the section)
SubjectsHistory, art and music, life sciences, physical sciences, business and economics, social sciences

Why this task matters most for a high score

On the harder (Upper) path, academic talks give you 12 of your 35 scored points: one talk in Module 1 and two in Module 2. No other task type carries that much weight. And because the talk comes at the end of Module 1, it also helps decide whether you reach the Upper module in the first place.

What the Screen Looks Like

  • A short line before the audio tells you the subject, for example Listen to a talk in a biology class. That is all the warning you get.
  • During the talk you see only the speaker. There are no slides, no key words and no diagrams on the screen, so your notes are your only record.
  • The four questions come one at a time. Usually the first asks about the main idea and the others about details, examples, organization or meaning.

What This Task Tests

Main and supporting ideas

What the whole talk is about, and which points and examples support it

Organization

How the speaker builds the explanation: definition, comparison, cause and effect, steps

Inferences

Conclusions that follow from what the speaker says

Academic and informal vocabulary

New terms explained in the talk, plus the colloquial and idiomatic language that lecturers use

How a Talk Is Built

A short academic talk has little time, so it follows a clear path. Most talks look like this:

Hook

A question, a personal story or an everyday situation: "Have you ever noticed…?", "Did you see that new movie…?" It gets attention, but it is not the main idea.

Topic and key term

"That's what scientists call…", "It's a type of…" Here the real subject appears. Write the term down.

Explanation with examples

How it works, why it happens, one or two examples or comparisons. Most detail and purpose questions come from here.

Twist or second idea

"Now, …", "But there's a problem…", "There's another type…" A contrast, a limitation or a second concept. Often tested.

Conclusion

A practical tip, a consequence or a return to the hook: "So next time you…" Good for inference and attitude questions.

Taking Notes on a Talk

For a talk, notes really matter: two minutes of speech and four questions, with nothing on the screen. The goal is not to write everything but to capture the skeleton: the topic, the key term, the main points and how they are connected.

Example notes for the example talk below

TOPIC: countershading = camo

dark top / pale belly (shark, penguin)

HOW: sun ↑ → top bright, belly shadow

contrast → 3D visible

CS cancels → looks flat

+ 2nd benefit: depends who looks

bird ↓ sees dark on dark

predator ↑ sees pale on bright

BUT limit: best open space, 1 light dir.

forest (patchy light) ✗ → spots/stripes

Useful abbreviations

→leads to, causes ↑ ↓increase, decrease; above, below =means, is defined as ≠is different from, is not vsin contrast with b/cbecause exexample w/ w/owith, without BUTa contrast or limitation (write it big) ✓ ✗works / doesn't work; true / false

Indent examples under the idea they illustrate. Later, when a question asks Why does the speaker mention the bird?, you look up and see which point the bird belongs to, and that point is the answer.

Common Traps

TrapWhat it looks like
The hook as the main ideaThe talk starts with a movie or a trip to the supermarket, and an option makes that the topic. The topic is the concept that the hook introduces.
An example as the main ideaSharks and penguins are examples; the talk is about the color pattern they share.
True, but not in the talkAn option that is a real scientific fact you may know, but the speaker never said it. Answer only from the talk.
Reversed relationshipCause and effect swapped, or X and Y swapped in a comparison.
What the example is vs. why it's thereFor a purpose question, an option correctly describes the example itself but gives the wrong reason for mentioning it.
Too strongalways, never, completely, the only when the speaker said often, less effective, one reason.

Strategies for Listen to an Academic Talk

  • Wait for the key term. Don't write down the hook in detail. Start real notes when you hear "that's called…" or "today we're looking at…".
  • Follow the signal phrases. First, another, now, but, so show the structure. Write BUT or 2nd in your notes whenever the direction changes.
  • Link every example to its point. Purpose questions ask why an example is there. The answer is always the idea it illustrates.
  • Use context for new words. Speakers usually explain terms right away ("in other words…"). If not, the next sentence often gives an example that shows the meaning.
  • Notice the speaker's attitude. Words like ironically, surprisingly, unfortunately, it does work, sort of reveal opinion and are the basis for attitude questions.
  • Forget what you know about the subject. The correct answer is what the speaker said or implied, even if you know more about the topic.
  • Train with real lectures. TED-Ed videos, university podcasts and YouTube explainers of 2–4 minutes are very close to this task. Practice writing the skeleton after each one.
Example

You hear:

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a biology class.

Have you ever noticed that many sea animals are dark on top and pale underneath? Sharks, penguins, lots of fish. That's not an accident. It's a type of camouflage called countershading.

Here's how it works. Sunlight comes from above, so normally the top of an animal's body is bright and the belly is in shadow. That contrast is what makes a three-dimensional shape easy to see. Countershading cancels it out: the dark back absorbs light, the pale belly reflects what little light reaches it, and the animal looks flat, almost invisible.

There's a second benefit, and it depends on who's looking. A bird flying above the ocean looks down at a dark fish against dark, deep water. A predator swimming below looks up at a pale belly against the bright surface. Either way, the fish blends in.

Now, countershading isn't perfect. It works best in open environments with light from one direction, like open water or grassland. In a forest, where light comes through the leaves in patches, it's much less effective. That's one reason forest animals often have spots or stripes instead.

1. What is the talk mainly about?

AHow a color pattern helps animals avoid being seen
BWhy sharks hunt near the surface of the ocean
CHow animals change color from season to season
DThe differences between ocean and forest animals

2. According to the speaker, what makes a three-dimensional shape easy to see?

ABright colors on the animal's back
BLight coming from several directions
CThe difference between a bright top and a shadowed underside
DThe movement of the animal through the water

3. Why does the speaker mention a bird flying above the ocean?

ATo give an example of an animal that uses countershading
BTo show that countershading works for viewers above and below
CTo explain why fish usually swim in deep water
DTo name the main predator of most fish

4. What can be inferred about animals with spots or stripes?

AThey are usually predators rather than prey.
BThey are dark on top and pale underneath.
CThey cannot survive in open grassland.
DTheir patterns suit places where light is uneven.
Answers + Explanation

Correct Answers: 1 – A, 2 – C, 3 – B, 4 – D

Explanation

1. Main idea. The whole talk explains countershading, a type of camouflage. A describes it without using the term.

  • B takes one example (sharks) and builds a topic that is never discussed.
  • C sounds scientific, but seasonal color change is not mentioned: the "true, but not in the talk" trap.
  • D: forests appear only at the end, as a limitation.

2. Factual. "That contrast is what makes a three-dimensional shape easy to see", where the contrast is between the bright top and the belly in shadow. C paraphrases it.

  • B is reversed: the speaker says light usually comes from one direction (above).
  • A and D are never said.

3. Purpose. The bird appears right after "a second benefit… depends on who's looking", together with the predator below. The two viewers show that the pattern works from both directions, so B is correct.

  • A is the "what vs. why" trap: the bird is the viewer, not an animal that uses countershading.
  • C and D misuse "deep water" and "predator".

4. Inference. Countershading fails where light comes "in patches", and "that's one reason forest animals often have spots or stripes instead". So spots and stripes work better where light is uneven: D.

  • B describes countershading, not spots and stripes.
  • C is too strong: the speaker says nothing about their survival in grassland.
  • A is never discussed.

Practice: 3 Academic Talks

Press Play and take notes. Each talk plays only once and lasts about a minute and a half; the four questions appear after it ends. After you answer all four, you can read the transcript and listen again.

Talk 1 of 3 · Loss leaders

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a business class.

Let's talk about a pricing strategy you've probably fallen for without realizing it. It's called a loss leader. A loss leader is a product that a store sells at a very low price, sometimes even below what the store paid for it. So the store loses money on every one it sells. Why would anyone do that? Well, think about why you go to a supermarket. You see an ad: milk at half price. You go in for the milk, but while you're there, you also pick up bread, some fruit, maybe a bag of coffee. Those other items are sold at normal prices, with a normal profit. The store expects the profit on your whole basket to be much bigger than the small loss on the milk. That's why loss leaders are usually things people buy often, like milk or eggs. It has to be something that brings customers through the door. Now, the strategy has a risk. Some shoppers, we call them cherry pickers, buy only the discounted item and leave. If too many customers do that, the store really does lose money. So stores often put the discounted products at the back of the shop, and customers have to walk past everything else to reach them.

1. What is the talk mainly about?

AHow supermarkets decide where to open new stores
BWhy milk and eggs have become more expensive
CA strategy of selling some products at a loss to attract customers
DThe reasons why some shoppers avoid discounts
Show answer

C. The speaker defines the loss leader, explains why stores use it and describes its risk. C sums up the whole talk. B misuses the examples (milk, eggs). D reverses the idea of cherry pickers, who actually look for discounts. A is never discussed.

2. Why does the speaker mention an ad for milk at half price?

ATo illustrate how a loss leader gets customers into a store
BTo show that milk is the most profitable product in a supermarket
CTo complain about misleading advertisements
DTo compare prices in different supermarkets
Show answer

A. The milk ad is the example that shows the strategy in action: you come for the cheap milk and buy other things. A gives this purpose. B is the opposite: the store loses money on the milk. C and D are never suggested.

3. According to the speaker, why are loss leaders usually products like milk or eggs?

AThey are cheap for stores to buy.
BThey are easy to place at the back of the shop.
CThey are rarely bought by cherry pickers.
DPeople buy them frequently.
Show answer

D. "Loss leaders are usually things people buy often", because they need to bring customers through the door. D says this in other words. A: the store may even sell below its own cost. B mixes in the detail about product placement. C is never stated.

4. What can be inferred about why stores place discounted items at the back?

AThe back of the shop is the easiest place to restock.
BStores hope customers will buy other products on the way.
CCherry pickers are not allowed in the front of the store.
DDiscounted items take up a lot of space.
Show answer

B. Customers "have to walk past everything else to reach them". The talk doesn't say why directly, but the logic of the strategy is that the more products shoppers see, the more they buy, so B is the reasonable inference. A and D are not supported. C is absurd.

Talk 2 of 3 · Spaced practice

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a psychology class.

When you have an exam coming up, what do you do? If you're like most students, you study a lot the night before. Psychologists have a name for that: massed practice, or, less formally, cramming. And it does work, sort of. You'll probably remember enough to get through the test the next morning. The problem is what happens a week later. Most of it is gone. The alternative is called spaced practice. Instead of studying for five hours in one evening, you study for one hour on five different days. Same total time, but the results are very different. In study after study, people who spaced their practice remembered far more a month later than people who crammed. Why? One explanation is that forgetting is actually useful. When you come back to the material after a few days, you've partly forgotten it, and the effort of remembering it makes the memory stronger. It's a bit like exercise: a muscle grows because it has to work hard. So here's the irony. Cramming feels effective because everything seems fresh, and spacing feels harder because you keep struggling to recall things. But that struggle is exactly what makes it work.

1. What is the main topic of the talk?

AHow to prepare for an exam the night before
BWhy spreading study over time improves long-term memory
CHow physical exercise affects the brain
DWhy students forget information during exams
Show answer

B. The talk contrasts cramming with spaced practice and explains why spacing leads to better long-term memory. B states this. A is only the hook. C takes the exercise analogy literally. D: the problem is forgetting a week later, not during the exam.

2. How does the speaker explain why spaced practice works?

ABy comparing memory to a muscle that grows stronger with effort
BBy describing her own experience as a student
CBy listing the steps of a famous experiment
DBy giving statistics about exam results
Show answer

A. "It's a bit like exercise: a muscle grows because it has to work hard". The speaker uses an analogy, so A is correct. She mentions studies in general, but gives no steps (C) or numbers (D). B is never done: the speaker talks about "you", not about herself.

3. According to the speaker, what is true about cramming?

AIt helps students remember material for a long time.
BIt takes more total time than spaced practice.
CIt can help students pass a test the next day.
DIt is recommended by most psychologists.
Show answer

C. "It does work, sort of. You'll probably remember enough to get through the test the next morning." C paraphrases this. A is the opposite: most of it is gone a week later. B: the speaker says the total time is the same. D is never said.

4. What is the speaker's attitude toward cramming?

AShe thinks it is the best method for difficult subjects.
BShe believes it is completely useless.
CShe has no clear opinion about it.
DShe thinks it is less effective than it seems.
Show answer

D. "Sort of" and "here's the irony: cramming feels effective" show that she sees it as weaker than students believe. D captures this. B is too strong: she admits it helps for the next day's test. A and C contradict her clear preference for spacing.

Talk 3 of 3 · Urban heat islands

Narrator: Listen to a talk in an environmental science class.

Cities are warmer than the countryside around them, sometimes by several degrees. Scientists call this the urban heat island effect, and there are a few reasons it happens. First, materials. Asphalt roads and dark roofs absorb a lot of the sun's energy during the day. Then, at night, they slowly release that heat back into the air, which is why the difference between the city and the countryside is often greatest after sunset, not at noon. Second, there's less vegetation. Trees and plants cool the air by releasing water through their leaves, a bit like sweating. Replace a park with a parking lot, and you lose that natural air conditioning. And third, cities produce their own heat: cars, factories, air conditioners pushing hot air outside. Ironically, the hotter a city gets, the more people use air conditioning, which makes the streets even hotter. So what can cities do? Some are painting roofs white, so they reflect sunlight instead of absorbing it. Others are planting trees along streets, or even putting gardens on top of buildings. These solutions are fairly cheap, and research suggests they can lower temperatures noticeably, especially at night.

1. What is the talk mainly about?

AThe history of air conditioning in cities
BWhy parks are more popular than parking lots
CHow weather in the countryside is changing
DThe causes of higher temperatures in cities and ways to reduce them
Show answer

D. The speaker gives three reasons for the urban heat island effect and then describes solutions. D covers both parts. The other options each take one small detail from the talk and turn it into the topic.

2. According to the speaker, when is the temperature difference between a city and the countryside often greatest?

AAt noon
BIn the early morning
CAfter sunset
DDuring the summer only
Show answer

C. "The difference… is often greatest after sunset, not at noon", because roads and roofs release the heat at night. A is the time the speaker explicitly rejects. B and D are not mentioned.

3. Why does the speaker mention sweating?

ATo explain how plants cool the air
BTo describe how people feel in hot cities
CTo give an example of a health risk
DTo compare cities with the human body
Show answer

A. Sweating is an analogy for how plants release water through their leaves and cool the air. A states its purpose. B and C connect sweating to people, which is not the point. D is too broad: only plants are compared to sweating, not cities.

4. How does the speaker organize the talk?

ABy telling the story of one particular city
BBy listing causes of a problem and then describing solutions
CBy comparing two opposing theories
DBy describing an experiment step by step
Show answer

B. The talk goes "first… second… third…" through the causes, then asks "So what can cities do?" and presents solutions. That is a problem–solution structure, B. No single city, competing theories or experiment is described.
