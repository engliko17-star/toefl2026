Method Questions

A Method question asks how the speaker presents information: how they explain an idea, introduce a topic, organize the talk or persuade the listener. Do they compare two things, go step by step, give an example, tell a story? You need to notice not only what is said, but the technique the speaker uses to say it.

What it asksThe way the speaker explains, organizes or supports information
Where it appearsListen to a Conversation · Listen to an Announcement · Listen to an Academic Talk
Especially common inAcademic talks, where explanations have a clear structure
Main skillRecognizing patterns of explanation: comparison, sequence, classification, analogy, examples

How to Recognize It

  • How does the speaker explain …?
  • How does the professor introduce the topic of …?
  • What approach does the speaker use to describe …?
  • How does the speaker organize the discussion of …?
  • How does the speaker support the claim that …?
  • How does the woman try to persuade the man?

In the Official Guide examples, the options begin with By + -ing: By comparing…, By describing… step by step, By giving an example of…

Purpose: why?

Why does the speaker mention a phone's low-power mode?

To help explain what happens during hibernation

Method: how?

How does the speaker explain hibernation?

By comparing it to a phone's low-power mode

The same moment in the talk can produce both questions. Purpose starts from the detail and asks what it is for. Method starts from the idea and asks what technique was used to explain it. Purpose Questions covers the other direction.

Two Levels of Method Questions

The whole recording

How does the speaker organize the talk?

The answer describes the skeleton: three types, a process in stages, a problem and its solutions, a history in time order.

One idea

How does the speaker explain why rainwater is acidic?

The answer describes the tool used for one point: an analogy, an example, a study, a contrast.

How Wrong Options Are Built

TrapHow it works
Right topic, wrong techniqueBy citing a study of bats when the speaker used an analogy. The topic is correct; the method is not.
A method used elsewhereThe speaker really does compare or list somewhere in the recording, but not for the idea the question asks about.
Mentioned, not usedSomething is mentioned (a map on the club website) but it is not how the point is made.
A technique that sounds typicalBy describing an experiment, By quoting an expert: common in lectures, but absent from this one.
Wrong structureTwo opposing theories for a talk with three categories; chronological for a talk that compares.

Strategies for Method Questions

  • Mark the technique in your notes. Next to a point, add a tag: ex (example), = like (analogy), vs (contrast), 1-2-3 (steps), study, Q (question to the audience).
  • Listen for structural cues. "Let's compare…", "Here's another example…", "First, …", "There are three types…" tell you the method directly.
  • Match the question's target. If it asks how the speaker explains X, look only at the part about X. A method from another part of the talk is a trap.
  • Check both halves of the option. By comparing it to a phone: the technique (comparing) and the content (a phone) must both be right.
  • For "organize" questions, recall your skeleton. If your notes have 1-2-3, the answer is probably categories or stages; if they have X | Y columns, it's a comparison.
Example

You hear:

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a geology class.

How does a limestone cave form? It starts with rain. As rainwater falls through the air and soaks through the soil, it picks up carbon dioxide and becomes slightly acidic, a bit like very weak soda water. Next, this water seeps down into cracks in limestone rock underground. Limestone dissolves in acid, so over thousands of years, the water slowly widens those cracks into channels. Then the channels join together and grow into larger passages and chambers. Finally, if the water table drops, the chambers drain and fill with air, and we have a cave we can walk into. Now, the famous stalactites hanging from cave ceilings form in almost the reverse way. When water drips into an air-filled cave, it releases carbon dioxide, and the dissolved limestone turns back into solid rock, one tiny layer at a time.

1. How does the speaker explain how a cave forms?

ABy describing the process in stages
BBy comparing caves in different countries
CBy telling a story about exploring a cave
DBy listing different types of rock

2. How does the speaker help listeners understand that rainwater is slightly acidic?

ABy describing a laboratory experiment
BBy giving exact measurements
CBy comparing it to a familiar drink
DBy describing its effect on plants

3. How does the speaker introduce the topic of stalactites?

ABy asking students where they have seen them
BBy defining several related terms
CBy giving an example of a famous cave
DBy contrasting how they form with how caves form
Answers + Explanation

Correct Answers: 1 – A, 2 – C, 3 – D

Explanation

1. Whole structure. It starts with…, Next…, Then…, Finally…: four clear stages. A.

  • B and C describe typical techniques that don't appear in this talk.
  • D: only limestone is discussed; no types of rock are listed.

2. One idea. "a bit like very weak soda water" is an analogy with something everyone knows. C.

  • A and B sound scientific, but there is no experiment and no number.
  • D: the water passes through soil, but its effect on plants is never mentioned.

3. Introducing a topic. "Now, the famous stalactites… form in almost the reverse way." The speaker presents them as the opposite of the process just described. D.

  • A: the talk does start with a question, but that was about caves, not stalactites. A method used elsewhere.
  • B and C are not in the talk.

Practice: 8 Method Questions

An announcement, two academic talks and a conversation. Each recording plays only once. While you listen, tag each point in your notes with its technique: ex, like, vs, 1-2-3, study, Q.

1 of 4 · Announcement

Narrator: Listen to an announcement on a university radio station.

Hi, everyone, this is Sam from Campus Recreation. As you know, the main gym is closed for renovation until March. I know a lot of you have been skipping workouts because of that, so here's some good news. The temporary gym in the old library basement now has twice as many machines as it did in the fall. And to make it easier, we've extended its hours: it's open until eleven p.m. every night. Just last week, over three hundred students checked in, and the feedback has been great. So give it a try!

1. How does the speaker encourage students to use the temporary gym?

ABy warning that the main gym will stay closed permanently
BBy describing improvements and how many students already use it
CBy offering free sessions with a personal trainer
DBy comparing it with gyms off campus
Show answer

B. He lists what's better (twice as many machines, longer hours) and adds that over three hundred students came last week. B. A contradicts the talk: the main gym reopens in March. C and D are techniques that never appear.

2. How does the speaker show that the temporary gym is popular?

ABy comparing it with the main gym
BBy quoting a student who uses it
CBy describing its convenient location
DBy giving the number of recent visitors
Show answer

D. "Over three hundred students checked in" is a number used as evidence. D. B: "the feedback has been great" is a summary, not a quote. C: the basement is mentioned but not presented as convenient. A is not done.

2 of 4 · Academic talk

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a biology class.

When winter comes, animals in cold climates face a serious problem: food becomes scarce and temperatures drop. Biologists generally group their solutions into three strategies. The first is to leave. Many birds migrate thousands of kilometers to warmer regions, and some butterflies do the same. The second strategy is to slow down. Hibernating animals, like certain bats, lower their body temperature and heart rate so much that they use very little energy. Think of it like putting a phone into low-power mode. It still works, but only the essential functions are running. The third strategy is to stay active and adapt. Snowshoe hares, for example, grow a thick white coat that keeps them warm and hides them in the snow. Now, a common belief is that bears hibernate. Technically, they don't, at least not in the same way as bats. A bear's body temperature drops only a few degrees, and it can wake up quickly if it's disturbed. Scientists usually call this a lighter form of winter sleep.

1. How does the speaker organize the talk?

ABy dividing animals' winter strategies into three groups
BBy following one animal through a whole year
CBy comparing winters on different continents
DBy presenting two opposing theories
Show answer

A. "Three strategies… The first… The second… The third…" is classification. A. B and C are structures that don't appear. D is the wrong structure: there are three strategies, not two competing theories.

2. How does the speaker explain what happens during hibernation?

ABy citing a study of bats
BBy describing the process step by step
CBy comparing it to a phone's low-power mode
DBy contrasting it with migration
Show answer

C. "Think of it like putting a phone into low-power mode" is an analogy. C. A is right topic, wrong technique: bats are an example, but no study is described. D: migration is the previous strategy, not used to explain hibernation.

3. How does the speaker deal with the idea that bears hibernate?

ABy telling a personal story about bears
BBy correcting a common belief with evidence
CBy agreeing and giving more examples
DBy asking students to research the question
Show answer

B. "A common belief is… Technically, they don't", followed by facts about body temperature and waking up. That's correcting a misconception with evidence. B. C is the opposite. A and D do not happen.

3 of 4 · Academic talk

Narrator: Listen to a talk in an economics class.

Here's a question: why do most of us get two days off at the end of the week? For most of history, people worked six days and rested on one, usually for religious reasons. The two-day weekend is surprisingly recent. In the early nineteen hundreds, some factories in the United States began giving workers Saturday afternoons off as well. Then, in nineteen twenty-six, Henry Ford made a bold decision: his factories would close on both Saturday and Sunday, with no cut in pay. Critics said productivity would fall. Instead, Ford argued, well-rested workers would work harder, and, just as important, people with free time would travel and shop, perhaps even buying cars. Within about fifteen years, a forty-hour, five-day week became law in the United States for many workers. So the weekend wasn't just a gift to workers. It was also, at least partly, a business strategy.

1. How does the speaker introduce the topic?

ABy quoting Henry Ford
BBy describing a modern factory
CBy defining the term productivity
DBy asking a question about something familiar
Show answer

D. The talk opens with "Here's a question: why do most of us get two days off…?" D. Ford appears later and is not quoted directly. B and C don't happen.

2. How does the speaker support the claim that the weekend was partly a business strategy?

ABy explaining Ford's reasons for his decision
BBy listing today's working hours in several countries
CBy comparing American and European factories
DBy showing that the critics' predictions came true
Show answer

A. Ford expected rested workers to work harder and free time to lead to more shopping, including buying cars. These business reasons support the final claim. A. D is the opposite: the speaker says "Instead", so the critics were not proven right. B and C are not in the talk.

4 of 4 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between two students.

Man: I don't know if I should take the summer internship. It's unpaid.

Woman: I get that. But do you remember my cousin Dana? She did the same internship two years ago.

Man: The one who works at the design studio now?

Woman: Right. She says they hired her because they already knew her work from the internship.

Man: Hmm. That does make it sound more worthwhile.

How does the woman try to persuade the man to take the internship?

ABy offering to lend him money
BBy listing the tasks he would do
CBy mentioning someone who benefited from the same internship
DBy comparing it with a paid summer job
Show answer

C. She tells him about her cousin, who got a job because of this internship. C. The man's reaction ("That does make it sound more worthwhile") shows the method worked. A, B and D are never used.
