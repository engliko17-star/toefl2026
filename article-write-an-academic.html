Write for an Academic Discussion

In this task you take part in an online class discussion. A professor writes a short post that introduces a topic and asks a question. Two classmates have already replied, usually with different opinions. You write your own post: you state your position and support it with reasons, examples or your own experience.

According to ETS, the task shows whether you can write a text that develops an argument for a position, reacting to other people's ideas and/or using information from the posts; is well supported, clear and easy to follow; uses a range of grammar and vocabulary accurately; and follows the rules of spelling, punctuation and capitalization.

What ETS research says

ETS researchers compared this 10-minute task with the old 30-minute Independent essay. 1,095 adult learners wrote both. Several findings are directly useful for test takers:

FindingWhat to do with it
47% got exactly the same score on both tasks, 50% differed by one point, and the average scores were not meaningfully different.The short post measures the same writing ability as a full essay. Everything that makes an essay good — a clear position, development, variety, accuracy — still matters.
Response length was less strongly related to the score than in the old essay.Do not pad your post. A focused 120–150 words with one developed reason beats a long list.
Posts reused words and short phrases from the prompt more often than essays did.Refer to the classmates' ideas, but paraphrase them. Your own wording is your score.
Higher scorers spent slightly more time planning, paused less while typing and deleted less.Spend the first minute deciding your position and your example, then write steadily instead of rewriting sentences.
Across both tasks, higher scorers showed richer vocabulary, more syntactic variety and fewer grammar, usage and spelling errors.Range and accuracy are what separate a 4 from a 5.

How it is scored

A post gets one holistic score from 0 to 5. ETS uses an AI scoring engine together with human raters, and human raters take over when the engine is not confident. Unlike the email, this rubric has no separate line for tone: it is about relevance, development and language.

15 lifehacks

1

Read the last sentence of the professor's post first.

That is the actual question. Everything else in the post is background. Knowing the question before you read the classmates saves time and keeps you on topic.
2

Recognize the question type in two seconds.

More than half of the questions ask you to choose between two options. Pick one clearly — or explain under what conditions each is better — but never leave the reader guessing where you stand.
55% of tasks · Platform analysis
3

Always answer “why”.

Half of the prompts literally say Why? or Why or why not?, and the rest expect it. A position without a reason is only a sentence.
51% of tasks · Platform analysis
4

Name a classmate and react to the idea.

ETS describes the task as responding to others' viewpoints. One sentence such as Tom is right that…, but… shows you are part of the discussion — and gives you a natural transition to your own point.
ETS Writing page
5

Add one thing nobody has said.

The two classmates have already used the obvious reasons. A new angle — a different group of people, a long-term effect, a practical compromise — is what makes your post a contribution.
6

Develop one reason instead of listing three.

Explain the reason, then show it with a specific example from your experience, your country or something you have read. One developed idea gets more credit than three bullet-like sentences.
7

Paraphrase — never copy — the posts.

Research shows that test takers reuse more prompt language in this task than in a classic essay, and the rubric describes 1-point posts as mostly borrowed language. Retell a classmate's idea in five new words.
ETS research blog · Appendix A
8

Aim for 120–150 words.

At least 100 words gives you room for a position, a reason and an example. Beyond that, extra length matters less than in the old essay, so stop when your point is complete.
ETS research blog
9

Plan for one minute, then type without stopping.

Higher scorers planned slightly longer and then paused less and deleted less. Decide your position, your new idea and your example before the first word.
ETS research blog
10

Use a personal example — it is allowed and effective.

According to ETS, test takers support their opinion with their own reasoning, experiences or knowledge. When I worked at a café last summer… is a perfect example if it really supports your point.
Technical Manual, II-5
11

Skip ready-made openers.

This is a very interesting and controversial topic adds words but no content. Start with your answer: In my view, …
12

Prepare vocabulary for the big four topics.

Business, society, education and technology cover three quarters of the tasks. Collect 10–15 precise words and collocations for each (work-life balance, remote work, curriculum, screen time).
75% of tasks · Platform analysis
13

Concede before you disagree.

Admittedly, …; however, … and Even though…, I still believe… make your argument sound balanced and add complex sentences — two birds with one stone.
14

Proofread the last minute.

Look at verb forms (he think), articles and plural -s. There is no spell checker.
15

Treat it like half of an essay, not a chat message.

The tone is academic but personal: I and contractions are fine, slang and emojis are not.

How to build your post

A clear post usually has four moves. They are not a template to memorize — the words should change with every topic — but they help you organize your thinking quickly:

MoveWhat it doesHow it can sound
1. PositionAnswer the professor's question in one sentence.In my view, city centers should be closed to private cars, but not to everyone.
2. Link to the discussionRefer to a classmate by name and react to the idea in your own words.Tom is right that some people cannot walk far. However, that is a reason to plan the zone well, not to keep the traffic.
3. Your new pointGive a reason nobody has mentioned and explain it.What is often forgotten is that… / Another way to look at this is…
4. ExampleMake the reason concrete: a place, a fact you know, a personal experience.When I lived in…, I saw that… / For instance, …
Useful language
  • Giving a view: In my view, … · I am convinced that … · I lean towards the idea that … · On balance, I think …
  • Agreeing with a classmate: Priya makes a good point about … · I share Tom's concern that …, and I would add that …
  • Disagreeing politely: I see why Tom feels that …, but … · That may be true in some cases; however, …
  • Adding a new idea: One thing neither of them mentions is … · There is another side to this: …
  • Explaining: This matters because … · As a result, … · This would mean that …
  • Giving an example: For instance, … · A good example is … · In my own experience, …
  • Conceding: Admittedly, … · Even though …, I still believe …

Examples

Example 1: Car-free city centers

Your professor is teaching a class on urban studies.

Dr. Alvarez: Over the last decade, many cities have turned the streets in their historic centers into pedestrian-only zones. Supporters say these areas become cleaner, safer and better for local businesses, while critics argue that they make life harder for people who depend on cars. What is your view? Should city centers be closed to cars? Explain your reasons.

Priya: Yes, I think they should. When my hometown closed its main square to traffic, the air felt cleaner almost immediately, and families started spending whole afternoons there. Streets without cars are simply more pleasant places to be.

Tom: I disagree. Not everyone can walk long distances. Older people, people with disabilities and parents with small children need to be able to drive close to where they are going. Closing the center to cars would shut them out of it.

Sample response (level 5) · 156 words
I believe city centers should be closed to private cars, but the change has to be planned around the people Tom is worried about. His point is important: if a pedestrian zone simply bans all vehicles, older residents and people with disabilities lose access to shops and doctors. However, that is a reason to design the zone carefully, not a reason to keep the traffic. What is often missing from this debate is the role of public transport. When the center of my city became car-free five years ago, the local government added small electric shuttles that stop every few minutes and accept wheelchairs. As a result, my grandmother, who cannot walk more than a few hundred meters, now goes downtown more often than before, because she no longer has to search for parking. A car-free center works when it replaces cars with something better, and that is exactly what cities should invest in.
Why it works
The first sentence answers the professor's question directly, and the second one engages with Tom by name instead of ignoring the opposite view. The writer then adds a new idea — public transport — that neither classmate mentioned and supports it with a concrete, personal example (the shuttles, the grandmother). Linking words such as However, As a result and because make the logic easy to follow, and the closing sentence ties the example back to the position.
Example 2: Courses outside your major — two versions

Your professor is teaching a class on education.

Dr. Chen: Many universities require students to take a number of courses outside their major — for example, an engineering student might have to take a course in literature or history. Some people think these requirements give students a broader education. Others believe students should focus on their own field. Should universities require courses outside a student's major? Why or why not?

Leah: I think they should. A philosophy class I had to take in my first year taught me how to build an argument, and I still use that skill in my economics essays. You never know which course will turn out to be useful.

Marcus: I don't think so. University is expensive, and every course takes time. Students already know what they want to study, so they should be able to spend all their credits on subjects that will help them in their careers.

Version A — level 3
I agree with Leah. Universities should require courses outside the major. It is good for students because they learn many things. For example they can learn history or art and it is interesting. Also it is good for the career because employers like people who know many things. Marcus says university is expensive but I think it is not a big problem. In conclusion, I think universities should require these courses because they are useful for students.
Version B — level 5
I agree with Leah, although I understand Marcus's concern about cost. In my opinion, the main value of these courses is not the extra knowledge but the chance to practice skills that a specialized program rarely trains. Take communication as an example. Engineering students spend most of their time solving technical problems, yet after graduation they constantly have to explain their ideas to clients and managers who are not engineers. A literature or history course forces them to write clear arguments and discuss them with people who think differently. My cousin, who is a software developer, told me that the presentation skills she gained in a required public speaking class helped her get promoted faster than her colleagues. If universities want to address Marcus's point, they could limit these requirements to two or three courses, but removing them completely would leave graduates less prepared for real jobs.
What makes the difference
Both versions agree with Leah, so the difference is in development and language. Version A lists general reasons (they learn many things, it is interesting) without explaining them, repeats it is good, answers Marcus with a simple denial and ends with a conclusion that adds nothing. Version B makes one precise claim — these courses train skills a major does not — and develops it step by step: a general explanation, a specific situation (engineers talking to clients) and an example from someone the writer knows. It also responds to Marcus with a practical compromise instead of dismissing him. The sentences are varied, and the vocabulary is exact (specialized program, forces them to, address Marcus's point).
