In this task you read a short situation from student or everyday life and write an email about it. The prompt tells you who the reader is — a professor, an office, a classmate, a friend — and gives you three things to do. According to ETS, typical purposes include making a request, giving information, recommending something, extending an invitation or proposing a solution to a problem.

How it is scored

A response gets one holistic score from 0 to 5. According to the Technical Manual, emails are scored by ETS's AI scoring engine together with human raters; when the engine is not confident about a response, a human rater scores it. The scoring guide looks at four things:

Rubric areaWhat the engine measures (examples from ETS)What it means for you
Content
elaboration that supports the purpose		number of sentences · discourse coherence · similarity to the promptCover every bullet, stay on topic, and develop each point in 2–3 connected sentences.
Language range
syntactic variety, idiomatic word choice		sentence variety · word frequency · correct collocationsMix sentence types; use natural word pairs (make a request, meet a deadline).
Social conventions
politeness, register, organization		politeness indicators such as modal verbs and hedge wordsCould you…? Would it be possible…? I was wondering if… are not decoration — they are scored.
Accuracy
almost no lexical or grammatical errors		grammar errors · word-usage errors · spelling and punctuationSave the last minute for proofreading. There is no spell checker.
ScoreWhat it usually looks like

Fully successful

Every point is developed with useful details, the tone fits the reader throughout, sentences are varied and words are precise. Errors are rare and minor.

Clear and easy to follow. Details are adequate, the tone is mostly right, and there are only a few errors.

15 lifehacks

1

Three bullets = three paragraphs, in the same order.

Every task has exactly three bullets. Give each one its own short paragraph and keep the task's order — the reader (and the coherence measures) can find each point immediately.
Platform analysis: 70 of 70 tasks
2

Underline every “and” before you start.

More than half of the tasks hide a second task inside one bullet: thank her and mention what helped most. The second half is the part students skip most often.
54% of tasks · Platform analysis
3

Translate each verb into a job.

describe = what, where, when (facts) · explain = why / so what (consequences) · suggest = a concrete idea + why it helps · ask / request = a real question with a question mark. Explain and describe alone make up almost half of all bullets.
Platform analysis: 210 bullets
4

Take the greeting from the To: line.

Title + surname for Dr. Lee or Ms. Parker (Dear Dr. Lee,), Hi + first name for Alex, Dear … Team, for an office. Never Ms. + first name. Two emails out of three go to someone with a title, so polite-neutral is your default tone.
69% titled recipients · Platform analysis
5

Invent details freely.

Order numbers, dates, room numbers, times, names of cafés — nobody checks them. A sentence with since October 3 or about forty minutes is more convincing than recently or a long time.
6

Use the facts of the situation, not its sentences.

Your email must stay on topic, but the rubric describes 1-point responses as language “mostly borrowed from the stimulus”, and a response copied from the prompt gets 0. Retell the situation in your own words — that is already a detail.
Technical Manual, Appendix A
7

Politeness is literally measured.

The engine counts politeness indicators such as modal verbs and softening words. Turn orders into questions: Fix it → Could you have a look at it?; I want → I would like / I was wondering if.
Technical Manual, Table 3
8

Length does not buy points — content does.

In our students' emails, the median length was about 105 words both for those who got 2.5 and for those who got 5. What separated them was details, tone and accuracy. Aim for 100–150 words: enough sentences to develop three points, not more.
Platform analysis of student emails
9

Write the frame first.

Type the greeting and the sign-off with your name before the body. If time runs out, the email still looks complete.
10

Know the three big email types by heart.

Problems, requests and feedback make up two-thirds of all tasks. Prepare a few natural phrases for each — for example, Unfortunately, …, Would it be possible to…?, One thing that could make it even better is…
67% of tasks · Platform analysis
11

Check spelling first, articles second.

In 387 teacher reviews of our students' emails, the most frequent comments were spelling and typos (39%), articles (30%) and tone (29%). Learn the words people misspell under pressure: sincerely, receive, separate, definitely, accommodation.
Platform analysis: 387 reviews
12

One error-free point beats one missing point.

A half-covered bullet (11% of review comments) costs more than a small grammar slip. If you have 30 seconds left and one bullet is missing, write one sentence for it before you proofread.
Platform analysis: 387 reviews
13

Contractions are fine — for the right reader.

I'm, don't, I'll sound natural to a friend or classmate. To a professor or an office, write them out and avoid exclamation marks.
14

No angry words, only facts.

Even a complaint should sound calm: describe what happened with numbers and ask for a solution. This is unacceptable!!! lowers the social-conventions part of the score; We waited forty minutes for our order does the job.
15

One email point = one Build a Sentence item.

The Writing raw score is 20 points: 10 for Build a Sentence and 5 for each written task. Each step on the 0–5 scale of the email is worth exactly as much as one sentence in Build a Sentence — so do not rush the email to “save time”.
Technical Manual, Table 2

Phrases to use

1. First line: why you are writing
Formal

I am writing about…

I am writing about the parking permit I applied for on March 3.
FormalRequest

I am writing to ask whether…

I am writing to ask whether the lab will be open during spring break.
Neutral

I wanted to let you know that…

I wanted to let you know that our presentation slides are ready for review.
Informal

Quick question about…

Quick question about Saturday: are we still meeting at ten?
InformalInvitation

Guess what?…

Guess what? I finally found a climbing gym near campus.

2. Background: what happened and why it matters
Neutral

Here is what happened:…

Here is what happened: the bus broke down halfway, and I reached the exam room twenty minutes late.
Neutral

The problem is that…

The problem is that the form closes at midnight, and the upload button does not respond.
FormalApology

I am sorry that…, and I understand that…

I am sorry that I missed the deadline, and I understand that it caused extra work for the team.
Neutral

As a result,… / This means that…

As a result, I cannot print my handouts before the seminar.
Informal

The thing is,…

The thing is, my shift at the bookstore ends at six.

3. Details that make the email specific
Neutral

For example,… / In particular,…

The talk was full of practical advice. In particular, the tip about short cover letters was new to me.
Neutral

What I liked most was…

What I liked most was the hands-on part, when we tested our own water samples.
Neutral

Numbers, times and names

The heater in Room 214 has been off since Monday; it is about 15°C by the evening.
Neutral

not only… but also…

The new route is not only shorter but also much safer for cyclists.
Neutral

Because of this,… / That is why…

The café is quiet in the morning. That is why I usually study there before class.

4. Requests, suggestions and offers
FormalRequest

Would it be possible to…?

Would it be possible to submit the report on Monday instead?
Formal

I was wondering if…

I was wondering if you could recommend a reading for beginners.
NeutralSuggestion

One option would be to…

One option would be to hold the meeting online, so nobody has to travel.
NeutralSuggestion

I would suggest + -ing…, because…

I would suggest adding a second session, because the first one filled up in a day.
InformalInvitation

How about…? / Do you want to…?

How about meeting at the main entrance at noon?
NeutralOffer

I would be happy to…

I would be happy to bring the extension cords if that helps.

5. Last line and sign-off
Formal

Thank you for your time and help.

Works for any email to a professor, an office or a manager.
Formal

Please let me know if you need any more information.

Useful when you report a problem or send documents.
Neutral

I look forward to hearing from you.

Use it when you expect an answer.
Informal

Let me know what you think!

Natural at the end of an invitation or a suggestion to a friend.
Informal

Can't wait to see you!

Only for close friends and family.

Sign-offs

Formal / neutral

Best regards, · Kind regards, · Sincerely,

Friendly

Best, · Thanks, · See you soon, · Talk soon,

Put a comma after the sign-off and your name on the next line. Phrases are tools, not a script: a response built mostly from ready-made sentences says little about your own English, so use them to connect your details.

Five common email types

Most prompts fall into one of these types. The three bullets usually match three short paragraphs:

Request
  • Paragraph 1: who you are (if needed) and what you need — I am a second-year biology student, and I am writing to ask whether…
  • Paragraph 2: the reason and the details — dates, names, why it matters to you.
  • Paragraph 3: the request itself as a question, plus an easy next step — Could you let me know by Friday? I can come to your office hours if that is easier.
Problem and solution
  • Paragraph 1: describe the problem with facts: what, where, since when.
  • Paragraph 2: explain the effect on you — what you cannot do because of it.
  • Paragraph 3: propose a concrete solution and say why it would work. A calm tone is more convincing than a strong complaint.
Recommendation
  • Paragraph 1: name the thing you recommend and describe it.
  • Paragraph 2: connect it to the reader — you mentioned that you…, so…
  • Paragraph 3: one practical tip: when to go, what to try, what to avoid.
Invitation
  • Paragraph 1: what the event or plan is.
  • Paragraph 2: why the person would enjoy it.
  • Paragraph 3: time, place and what to bring, then a question: Are you free on Sunday?
Thanks and feedback
  • Paragraph 1: thank the person for something specific.
  • Paragraph 2: what worked well or what you learned — one clear example.
  • Paragraph 3: one polite suggestion: One thing that might make it even better is…

Examples

The tasks and responses below were written for this platform in the style of the test. Example 2 shows two responses to the same task so you can compare a middle and a top score.

Example 1: The missing charger (formal)

For a class project, you borrowed a video camera from the campus media lab. When you returned it yesterday, the lab assistant noticed that the battery charger was not in the case. You have searched your room but have not found it yet.

Write an email to Mr. Ortiz, the media lab manager. In your email, do the following:

  • Apologize and explain what happened to the charger.
  • Describe what you have done to find it.
  • Propose a way to solve the problem.
Sample response (level 5)
To: Mr. Ortiz Subject: Missing charger for camera #12 Dear Mr. Ortiz, I am writing about camera #12, which I returned to the media lab yesterday afternoon. I am very sorry, but the battery charger was not in the case. I used the camera for a group interview in the library on Monday, and I think the charger was left in the study room when we packed up in a hurry. Since then, I have checked my room and my backpack twice, and this morning I asked at the library's lost and found desk. Unfortunately, nobody has handed it in so far. If the charger does not turn up by Friday, I would be glad to buy a new one of the same model and bring it to the lab. Alternatively, I could pay the replacement fee, whichever is easier for you. Thank you for your patience, and again, I apologize for the trouble. Best regards, Lena Morozova
Why it works
Each point has its own paragraph. The apology is followed at once by a believable explanation (the group interview, packing in a hurry), so it does not sound empty. The second paragraph lists real actions — the room, the backpack, the lost and found desk. The solution gives a deadline and two options, which makes it easy for Mr. Ortiz to say yes. Polite, formal phrases (I would be glad to…, whichever is easier for you) suit a staff member, and the subject line names the exact item.
Example 2: Advice for a cousin (informal) — two versions

Your cousin Mia will move to your city next month to start university. She has asked you which part of the city would be a good place to rent a room.

Write an email to Mia. In your email, do the following:

  • Recommend an area and explain why it would suit her.
  • Describe one thing she should be careful about.
  • Offer to help her in some way.
Version A — level 3
Hi Mia, I think you should live in Riverside. It is a good area. It is near the university and it is cheap. There are many shops and cafes. You should be careful with the rent. Some rooms are expensive. Also be careful with the landlord. I can help you. Tell me when you come. Bye, Kate
Version B — level 5
Hi Mia, I'm so glad you're moving here! If I were you, I'd look for a room in Riverside. It's only a ten-minute bike ride from campus, and rooms there usually cost about a third less than in the city center. It's also a lively area full of students, so you'll find cheap cafés and a big supermarket that stays open until midnight. One thing to watch out for: some landlords there ask for three months' rent in advance. Don't pay anything until you've seen the room in person and signed a proper contract. If you like, I can go and see a few places for you before you arrive and send you photos and videos. Just send me the links! Talk soon, Kate
What makes the difference
Both versions cover all three points, so the difference is not the plan but the elaboration and range. Version A states general opinions (it is a good area, it is cheap) without saying how near or how cheap, and it uses the same short pattern in almost every sentence. The warning about the landlord is too vague to be useful. Version B answers the reader's real question with facts — ten minutes by bike, a third cheaper, a supermarket open until midnight — gives a concrete warning with advice, and makes an offer Mia can act on. Contractions, If I were you and an exclamation mark make the tone warm and natural for family.
Example 3: A crowded career panel (semi-formal)

Your department organized a career panel where three former students talked about their first jobs. You found the event useful, but it was held in a small seminar room, and many students had to stand at the back.

Write an email to Ms. Park, the event coordinator. In your email, do the following:

  • Thank her and mention something you learned at the panel.
  • Describe the problem with the room.
  • Suggest how future panels could be improved.
Sample response (level 5)
To: Ms. Park Subject: Thank you for Thursday's career panel Dear Ms. Park, Thank you for organizing Thursday's career panel. I found it very practical, especially when one of the speakers explained how she turned a summer internship into a full-time job offer. I had never thought of asking for feedback before an internship ends, and I am definitely going to try it. The only problem was the room. Seminar Room B has about thirty chairs, but I think more than fifty students came, so many of us stood at the back for an hour and could hardly see the slides. Since the event was so popular, would it be possible to book the lecture hall next time? Another idea would be to record the talks, so that students who cannot attend could watch them later. Best regards, Daniel Kim
Why it works
The thanks is specific: the reader learns exactly which advice was helpful and what the writer plans to do with it. The problem is described with numbers instead of complaints, and it is introduced with The only problem was…, which keeps the positive tone. The suggestions are phrased as polite questions and ideas and are linked to the reason (Since the event was so popular…).
