Inference Questions

An Inference question asks about something the speaker means but doesn't say directly. People do this all the time: "I've got a lab until five" means "I can't come"; "Let's just say I've had better days" means "It went badly". To answer, you combine what you heard with the context and the speaker's tone and draw a logical conclusion.

What it asksA conclusion that follows from what is said: an implied meaning, an opinion, a likely next action
Where it appearsListen to a Conversation · Listen to an Announcement · Listen to an Academic Talk
Especially common inConversations, where people speak indirectly
Main skillUnderstanding intent, tone and context, and not going further than the evidence

How to Recognize It

  • What does the speaker imply about …?
  • What can be inferred about …?
  • What does the woman suggest about …?
  • What can be concluded from …?
  • What is the professor likely to do next?
  • What will the man probably do next?
  • What does the woman mean when she says, "…"?
  • What is probably true about …?

Key words: imply, infer, suggest, conclude, probably, likely, and "mean when… says". They tell you that the answer is not in the recording word for word.

Four Kinds of Inference

1 · What a phrase really means

What does the man mean when he says, "Let's just say I've had better days"?

Idioms, polite refusals, understatement, jokes. The answer is the meaning in this situation, not the literal one.

2 · A conclusion about someone or something

What can be inferred about the woman?

From details like "Last year I brought cookies from the supermarket, and I'm still embarrassed", you conclude what she wants this time.

3 · What will happen next

What will the man probably do next?

The answer is in the last lines: a plan, an agreement, an offer that was accepted.

4 · Connecting two pieces

What can be inferred about the first-floor printers?

Second-floor printers are broken + expect a line → many students will be using the first-floor ones.

"What will… probably do next?" is not always an inference

If the speaker says the plan directly ("I'll email it to you now"), the Official Guide calls it a Factual question. If you have to figure it out ("Grab a plate. I need an honest opinion" → he will taste the food), it's an Inference. For you, the approach is the same: look at the last lines.

The One-Step Rule

A correct inference is one short, safe step away from what was said. Every native speaker would draw the same conclusion. If you need a chain of "maybe… and then… so probably…", the option goes too far.

You hear: "The printers on the second floor are out of order. The first-floor printers are available, though you should expect a line, especially in the evenings."

Just repeats
The second-floor printers are broken. — true, but it answers nothing new
One step ✓
Many students will be using the first-floor printers.
Too far
Students will miss their assignment deadlines. — possible, but nothing supports it
Contradicts
The first-floor printers are rarely used. — the opposite of "expect a line"

Where the Clues Are

ClueExampleWhat it suggests
Hesitation"Hmm… yeah, I'll try."Not a firm yes
Contrast words"I'd love to, but…", "Actually, …"A refusal or a correction is coming
Excuse instead of an answer"I've got a report due Monday."Probably can't come
Past experience"That's what I said last weekend…"It didn't work out last time

How Wrong Options Are Built

TrapHow it works
Too farA conclusion that could be true, but needs extra assumptions. Words like entirely, always, never, all are warning signs.
The literal meaningFor "mean when… says" questions: the dictionary meaning of the phrase. "I've had better days" → He is ill.
The oppositeReverses the speaker's attitude or the logic of the situation.
General knowledgeSomething that is true in the world or typical of the situation, but nothing in the recording points to it.
The first plan, not the final oneFor "next" questions: what the speaker wanted at the beginning, before changing their mind.
Wrong personA conclusion about the other speaker.

Strategies for Inference Questions

  • Ask "What does this person really mean?" after every indirect or surprising line. Note your interpretation briefly: "prob. can't come", "annoyed".
  • Listen to the voice, not only the words. Hesitation, sarcasm, surprise and enthusiasm often decide the answer.
  • Find the evidence for each option. For the correct one, you should be able to point to the line it comes from. If you can't, it is probably "too far".
  • Prefer careful options. Correct inferences usually sound modest: may, some, often, is likely to. Extreme wording is more often wrong.
  • For "next" questions, trust the ending. The last two lines contain the decision. Earlier plans are traps.
Example

You hear:

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between two friends.

W: Hey, are you coming to Maya's birthday dinner on Saturday?

M: I'd love to, but I've got that marketing report due Monday, and I've barely started.

W: Oh, come on. It's just a couple of hours. You'll work better after a break anyway.

M: That's what I said last weekend, and I ended up writing until three in the morning on Sunday.

W: Fair enough. Should I tell Maya you'll at least stop by for dessert?

M: Hmm… yeah, tell her I'll try.

1. What does the man mean when he says, "That's what I said last weekend"?

ATaking a break before a deadline didn't work out for him last time.
BHe went to a dinner with Maya last weekend.
CHe usually finishes his reports early.
DHe agrees that breaks help him work better.

2. What will the man probably do on Saturday?

ASpend the whole evening at the birthday dinner
BWork on his report and possibly join for dessert
CHelp the woman organize Maya's birthday
DHand in his report before the deadline
Answers + Explanation

Correct Answers: 1 – A, 2 – B

Explanation

Question 1 (what a phrase means). The woman says a break will help. The man answers that he said the same last weekend and then had to write until 3 a.m. He never says "it was a mistake", but the story makes it clear: the break didn't help, it hurt. A.

  • D is the opposite: he is disagreeing with her, not agreeing.
  • B is too far: we don't know what he did last weekend, only that he took a break.
  • C contradicts the conversation: he has "barely started".

Question 2 (what happens next). The ending decides it: he agrees to "try" to come for dessert, and the hesitation ("Hmm… yeah") shows it isn't certain. Meanwhile, the report is clearly his priority. B combines both.

  • A is the first plan the woman proposed, which he rejected.
  • D goes too far: the report is due Monday, and he has barely started.
  • C is never suggested.

Practice: 8 Inference Questions

Two conversations, an announcement and an academic talk. Each recording plays only once. Listen for tone and indirect lines, and for every answer ask yourself: which line supports it?

1 of 4 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between two roommates.

Man: Something smells amazing. What are you making?

Woman: Just a vegetable curry. I'm testing a recipe for international night at the student center.

Man: International night? Is that the event where everyone brings a dish from home?

Woman: Exactly. Last year I brought cookies from the supermarket, and I'm still embarrassed about it.

Man: Well, if this tastes as good as it smells, you won't have that problem this time. Is there enough for two?

Woman: Grab a plate. I need an honest opinion anyway.

1. What can be inferred about the woman?

AShe is organizing international night.
BShe does not usually enjoy cooking.
CShe wants to bring something better than last year.
DShe works at the student center.
Show answer

C. She is still embarrassed about bringing store-bought cookies, and now she is testing a homemade recipe. The one-step conclusion: she wants to do better this time. C. A and D misuse "student center". B is not supported: nothing suggests she dislikes cooking.

2. What will the man probably do next?

ATaste the woman's curry
BGo to the supermarket
CCook a dish of his own
DSign up for international night
Show answer

A. He asks if there's enough for two, and she answers "Grab a plate. I need an honest opinion." He is going to try the curry. A. B misuses the supermarket from last year's story. C and D are not suggested.

2 of 4 · Announcement

Narrator: Listen to an announcement at a university library.

Good afternoon. This is a reminder from the library help desk. As many of you know, the printers on the second floor have been out of order since Monday. A technician is working on them, but the replacement parts won't arrive until next week. In the meantime, the printers on the first floor are available, though you should expect a line, especially in the evenings. If your printing is not urgent, you might want to wait until the weekend. Thank you for your patience.

1. What can be inferred about the printers on the first floor?

AThey are more expensive to use.
BMany students will be using them.
CThey will be replaced next week.
DThey only work in the evenings.
Show answer

B. The second-floor printers are broken, and the speaker warns about a line at the first-floor ones. Connecting the two: more people will use them. B. C belongs to the second-floor printers (parts arrive next week). D distorts "especially in the evenings". A is not supported.

2. What does the speaker suggest about printing on the weekend?

AThe library will be closed.
BIt will be free of charge.
CThe second-floor printers will be working again.
DIt will probably be less crowded.
Show answer

D. She advises waiting until the weekend if printing isn't urgent. The only logical reason: fewer people and shorter lines then. D. C contradicts the timing: parts arrive next week. A would make the advice meaningless. B is not mentioned.

3 of 4 · Academic talk

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a history class.

In the late sixteen hundreds, coffeehouses spread rapidly across London. For the price of a single penny, which covered your cup of coffee, you could sit for hours and join the conversation. And what conversations they were. Merchants, scientists, writers and politicians sat at the same long tables, reading newspapers aloud and arguing about the news. That's why people started calling coffeehouses penny universities. You could learn more there in an afternoon, some said, than in a year of formal study. Different coffeehouses attracted different crowds. At one, you'd find ship owners exchanging information about cargo and weather. That coffeehouse eventually grew into one of the world's biggest insurance markets. At another, you'd find people discussing the latest scientific experiments. Not everyone was happy about all this, though. In sixteen seventy-five, the king issued an order to close all the coffeehouses. The public reaction was so strong that the order was withdrawn after just eleven days.

1. What does the speaker imply about the learning that took place in coffeehouses?

AIt was more expensive than formal education.
BIt was only available to scientists.
CIt completely replaced universities in London.
DIt was informal but valuable.
Show answer

D. People of many professions learned by talking and reading the news, and some said you could learn more there than in a year of study. That suggests informal but valuable learning: D. C goes too far: "some said" is not "completely replaced". A is the opposite: it cost a penny. B contradicts the mix of merchants, writers and politicians.

2. What can be inferred about the king's view of coffeehouses?

AHe visited them regularly.
BHe saw them as a possible threat.
CHe wanted coffee to be cheaper.
DHe supported their growth.
Show answer

B. He ordered all of them closed, right after the speaker says "not everyone was happy". One step: he considered them a problem or a danger. B. D is the opposite. A and C have no support.

3. What can be concluded from the public reaction to the king's order?

AThe king rarely changed his decisions.
BMost coffeehouses closed permanently.
CCoffeehouses had become very important to many people.
DCoffee became more popular than newspapers.
Show answer

C. The reaction was strong enough to cancel a royal order in eleven days, so people cared a lot about coffeehouses. C. B contradicts the talk: the order was withdrawn. A is the opposite of what happened here. D is not supported.

4 of 4 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between two friends.

Woman: So, how was the job interview?

Man: Well, the first half went great. Then they asked me to solve a problem on the whiteboard.

Woman: And?

Man: Let's just say I've had better days.

Woman: Oh no. I'm sure it wasn't as bad as you think.

What does the man mean when he says, "Let's just say I've had better days"?

AThe problem-solving part went badly.
BHe is not feeling well today.
CThe interview was moved to another day.
DHe enjoyed the whole interview.
Show answer

A. "I've had better days" is an understatement that means something went wrong. It comes right after the whiteboard problem, and the woman tries to comfort him. A. B is the literal-meaning trap. D is the opposite. C misuses "days".
