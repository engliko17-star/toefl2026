The Speaking Section

The Speaking section measures two things. First, your foundational speaking skills: can you hear an English sentence and say it back clearly and accurately? Second, your ability to communicate: can you answer questions about your experiences and opinions in a natural, well-supported way?

You speak into a microphone, and your answers are recorded and scored. The section is short and intense: there is no preparation time. Every question is heard, not read, and you have to start speaking almost immediately, just as in a real conversation.

No Modules: a Linear Section

Unlike Reading and Listening, the Speaking section is not adaptive. There is no router module and no upper or lower path. Everyone who takes the same test form gets exactly the same tasks, in the same order, whatever their answers.

Reading & Listening

Module 1 → Upper Lower

Your first module decides the difficulty of the second.

Speaking

Listen and Repeat · 7 → Interview · 4

One fixed path for everyone.

What this means for you:

  • Difficulty grows inside each task, not between modules. Sentences 1–2 are short and simple; 6–7 are long and complex. Interview question 1 is about you; question 4 asks for an opinion on a broader issue.
  • A weak answer doesn't change what comes next. If you struggle with one sentence, the next one won't get easier. And a great answer won't make the next one harder. Treat each question as a fresh start.
  • Your score is based on all 11 responses together. One bad answer lowers the total a little; it doesn't decide your result.

How the Section Runs

Everything is automatic. You don't click Next: the test moves on by itself when your time is over.

Listen and Repeat · each of 7 sentences

Intro: scenario read aloud (once) → Sentence plays → ≈ 2 s pause → Beep → You speak: 8 / 10 / 12 s → "Response saved" → next

Take an Interview · each of 4 questions

Intro: situation read aloud (once) → Interviewer asks → You speak at once: 45 s → "Response saved" → next

The two tasks start differently

In Listen and Repeat, you wait for the beep; if you start too early, the beginning of your answer may not be recorded. In the interview, there is no beep and no pause: the timer starts as soon as the question ends. Start speaking right away, even with a simple opening phrase.

What the Screens Look Like

  • There are no Back, Next or Review buttons: the test moves forward by itself.
  • Each sentence and each question is heard once. There is no replay and no second recording.
  • When the time is up, a message says your response is being saved, and the next question starts automatically.

How Speaking Is Scored

  1. Every response gets 0–5 points, using a separate scoring guide for each task. Responses are scored holistically: one score for the overall quality, not a list of deductions.
  2. Scoring combines AI scoring engines and human raters. The engine is trained on human-scored responses and checked for accuracy and fairness.
  3. All 11 scores are added into a raw score from 0 to 55, which is then converted to the 1–6 band (in steps of 0.5). The conversion adjusts for small differences in difficulty between test forms.

Where the raw points come from

Listen and Repeat · 7 × 5 = 35
Interview · 4 × 5 = 20

In raw points, Listen and Repeat accounts for 35 of the 55 points. ETS converts the raw total to the 1–6 band with a statistical linking procedure; the exact weighting is not published.

Although each task has its own scoring guide, responses are generally judged on three features:

Delivery

How clear and fluent is your speech? Pronunciation, pace, pauses, rhythm, stress and intonation.

Language use

How accurately and effectively do you use grammar and vocabulary? Range matters, not just correctness.

Elaboration

In the interview: how well do you answer the question and support your ideas with reasons and examples?

Perfection is not expected

The scoring guides say that even top-scoring responses can have occasional errors, pauses and fillers like um or like. This is spontaneous speech. What matters is that you keep going, stay clear and say something meaningful.

The Rules to Remember

No preparation time

You respond right after the prompt. The tasks are designed to capture spontaneous speech.

Note-taking is not recommended

There's no time to use notes. Writing while listening only distracts you.

Everything happens once

Each prompt plays once, and you record once. No replay, no retry.

No memorized answers

In the interview, responses that seem to use memorized language receive a lower score. Speak in your own words.

General strategies for the Speaking section

  • Speak at a normal volume and a natural pace. Not too fast to sound fluent, not too slow to be careful. Clear speech at a steady pace scores better than rushed speech.
  • Never stop in the middle. In both tasks, partial and imperfect answers earn points; silence earns nothing. If you make a mistake, keep going.
  • Let each question go. The section is linear: a bad answer doesn't affect the next question. Reset and focus.
  • Practice with a timer and a recorder. Record yourself, listen back and compare. You will hear pauses and mispronunciations that you don't notice while speaking.
  • Shadow native speakers. Repeat sentences from podcasts, audiobooks or videos right after the speaker. It trains both tasks at once: memory for sentences and natural rhythm.
  • Check your equipment. Use a headset with a microphone if you can, and keep the microphone close to your mouth but not touching it.

Guides to Interview Question Types

The interview uses seven kinds of questions in two groups. Early questions are usually personal; later ones are abstract or speculative.

Personal questions

  • Personal experiences
  • Plans, desires and goals
  • Preferences and interests
  • Observations about people close to you

Abstract and speculative questions

  • Opinions on topics and trends
  • Speculating about the future
  • Hypothetical questions
