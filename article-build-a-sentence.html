Build a Sentence

In this task you see a short exchange between two people. The first person's line is complete; the second person's reply is broken into words and short phrases in the wrong order. You put the pieces into the gaps so that the reply is a grammatical sentence or question that fits the conversation.

According to ETS, the task measures your command of English sentence structure — a skill that every kind of writing depends on.

What is tested most often

These structures came up most often in our analysis:

StructureExampleWhat is tested
Indirect questionsCan you tell me where the meeting is?After Do you know / Can you tell me / I wonder the word order is like a statement: no do/does/did, subject before verb.
Direct questionsHow long have you been living here?Question word → auxiliary → subject → verb; prepositions often go to the end (Who did you go with?).
Relative clausesThat's the café that opened last week.who / that / where / whose right after the noun it describes.
Present perfectI haven't finished it yet.have/has + third form; already, just in the middle, yet at the end.
Verb patternsShe asked me to send the file.want / ask / need + to; enjoy / suggest / avoid + -ing.
Passive voiceIt is being repaired this week.be + third form; an -ing piece is often the trap.
Negatives and adverbsI don't usually eat breakfast.Position of not, never, still, usually.

15 lifehacks

1

Start from the end of the line.

The final punctuation is already shown. A question mark means a question — unless the line starts with I wonder / I'm not sure, or the question is embedded after Do you know / Can you tell me.
2

Use what is already written.

In almost four items out of five, part of the sentence is fixed. A fixed word tells you exactly what comes before and after it — often it removes every other option.
78% of items · Platform analysis
3

Read speaker A as a clue.

Two out of three first lines are questions. If A asks Where…?, your sentence probably contains a place; if A shares news, your sentence reacts or asks a follow-up.
66% · Platform analysis
4

Count pieces and gaps first.

One piece more than the gaps means there is an extra piece. This happens in about one item in six — so in a set of ten, expect one or two.
16% · Platform analysis
5

Suspect do / does / did first.

The most frequent extra piece is did, followed by other auxiliaries (do, does, am, was, will) and question words (when, where, why). Embedded questions never need do / does / did.
Platform analysis of extra pieces
6

Master indirect questions — they are almost a third of the test.

Do you know where the office is? — subject before verb, no auxiliary. If you learn one structure before the exam, learn this one.
~30% · Platform analysis
7

Subject + verb first, everything else after.

Find who does what and put those pieces together. Objects, places and times attach around them: She | sent | me | the file | last night.
8

Two-word pieces are hints.

A third of the pieces are phrases like the bus stop, going to or has been. They never split, and they often reveal the structure: has been → present perfect, is being → passive.
33% of pieces · Platform analysis
9

Prepositions love the end of questions.

Who are you going with? Which building is it in? What is it about? If a preposition is left over, try it at the end.
10

Adverbs have fixed seats.

Usually, never, always go before the main verb but after be; already, just go between have and the past participle; yet goes at the end.
11

Lowercase does not mean “not first”.

All pieces are shown in lowercase (except I and names), and the first word is capitalized automatically. Do not look for a capital letter to find the start.
12

Budget about 35 seconds per sentence.

The whole section takes about 23 minutes. If you leave around 17 minutes for the email and the discussion, ten sentences get about six minutes. Skip a hard one with Review and come back.
Our estimate
13

Re-read before you move on.

One wrong piece turns the whole sentence into 0. Read the full sentence once, silently, with speaker A's line before it.
All-or-nothing scoring · Technical Manual
14

Treat it as the cheapest points in the section.

Ten sentences give half of the Writing raw score. A student who fixes indirect questions and auxiliaries can gain several raw points faster than by improving an essay.
Technical Manual, Table 2
15

Train with mixed sets, not only by topic.

In the test, structures come in random order. After practicing each level of the trainer, finish with the Exam Mix to get used to switching.

Examples

The items below were written for this platform in the style of the test. Try each one before opening the answer.

Example 1: Lost keys (indirect question)
I think I left my keys in the lecture hall.
Do you
?
is / where / know / the lost-and-found / office / does
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: Do you know where the lost-and-found office is?

After Do you know, the question Where is the office? becomes an embedded question, so it takes statement order: where + subject (the lost-and-found office) + verb (is). The verb goes to the very end.

Extra piece: does. Embedded questions never use do / does / did — and the sentence already has Do at the start.

Example 2: A new roommate (direct question)
My new roommate arrived from Brazil yesterday.
from?
which / city / she / is / originally / are
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: Which city is she originally from?

A direct wh-question: the question phrase which city comes first, then the verb is before the subject she. The adverb originally goes before from, and the preposition stays at the end, as in Where is she from?

Extra piece: are — the subject she needs is.

Example 3: A recommendation (relative clause)
Is there a good place to study near campus?
café
.
there / is / a quiet / that / stays open / until / midnight
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: There is a quiet café that stays open until midnight.

The sentence answers the question with There is + noun phrase (a quiet café). The relative clause that stays open until midnight comes directly after the noun it describes. The word café is already in place, so a quiet must go right before it.

No extra piece here: seven pieces, seven gaps.
