Rhetorical Purpose

Rhetorical Purpose questions ask why the author included something: an example, a comparison, a number, a quotation, a question, or a whole paragraph. The answer is not what the information says but what job it does in the text. Some questions in this group also ask how paragraphs relate to each other or how the passage is organized.

What the question tests

Writers include information for a reason. An example illustrates a point; a study supports a claim; a contrast shows a difference; a fact can also weaken someone else's claim. This question checks whether you see the role a piece of information plays in the larger discussion.

Typical wording

  • Why does the author mention …?
  • Why does the author compare … to …?
  • Why does the author discuss the example of …?
  • Why does the author include the quotation from …?
  • Why does the author ask …?
  • What is the main purpose of paragraph 2?
  • How is paragraph 3 related to paragraph 2?
  • Which of the following best describes how the passage is organized?

Strategies for Rhetorical Purpose Questions

Step 1: Find the mention and the claim it serves.

Locate the detail the question names. Then find the statement it supports: usually the sentence just before it or the topic sentence of the paragraph.

Step 2: Ask "why," not "what."

Do not ask what the detail says. Ask what it does for that claim: does it illustrate, prove, contrast, explain, or challenge?

Step 3: Check both halves of the option.

A correct option has the right function verb and the right claim: To illustrate + how plants defend themselves. Many wrong options get one half right.

Step 4: Use the signal words.

For example means an illustration is coming; however means a contrast or a challenge; as a result means an effect.

Step 5: For structure questions, summarize each paragraph.

Give each paragraph a short label (P1 = popular belief; P2 = evidence against it). The relationship between the labels is your answer.

Common Traps

  • True content, wrong function. The option says something correct about the passage but describes the wrong job. This is the most common trap.
  • Right function, wrong claim. To give an example of … is correct, but the example supports a different point.
  • Too strong a verb. To prove, to argue that … is wrong when the author only illustrates or suggests.
  • Describing instead of explaining. An option that simply repeats what the detail says does not answer why.
Example 1: why is this detail here?

How Plants Defend Themselves

Plants cannot run away from the animals that eat them, but many of them are far from defenseless. Some defend themselves in surprisingly active ways. When caterpillars begin to chew the leaves of a tomato plant, for example, the plant starts producing chemicals that make its leaves harder for insects to digest. Other plants rely on simpler protection, such as thorns, tough bark, or a bitter taste.

1. Why does the author mention tomato plants?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

Look at the sentence just before: Some defend themselves in surprisingly active ways. The tomato sentence starts with for example, so its job is to illustrate that claim. The answer describes a function (to give an example) and the claim it serves (active defense).

  • Choice A – the passage does not say caterpillars prefer tomatoes.
  • Choice C – people are not mentioned; the chemicals affect insects.
  • Choice D – thorns appear in the next sentence as a different kind of protection, but the tomato example is not there to compare with them.
Example 2: how two paragraphs are connected

Goldfish Memory

Many people believe that goldfish can remember things for only a few seconds. The idea is repeated so often that it is used to explain why a fish in a small bowl never seems bored.

Experiments, however, tell a different story. In several studies, goldfish were trained to respond to a sound or a light in order to receive food, and many of them still responded correctly weeks or even months later.

1. How is paragraph 2 related to paragraph 1?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

Paragraph 1 describes a popular belief. Paragraph 2 begins with however and a different story, and then describes experiments in which goldfish remembered for weeks or months. That is evidence against the belief.

  • Choice A – the experiments contradict the belief; they do not support it.
  • Choice C – paragraph 2 says nothing about why people believe this.
  • Choice D – paragraph 1 describes a belief, not a problem that needs solving.
Example 3: a Daily Life review
★★★★★  Corner Bean Café

The coffee here is excellent, but the real reason I keep coming back is the staff. Last week, when a storm started just as I was leaving, the barista lent me her own umbrella and told me to bring it back "whenever." Where else does that happen?

1. Why does the writer mention the umbrella?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: C

Explanation

The writer first says the real reason to come back is the staff, then tells the umbrella story. The story is an example that supports that point.

  • Choice A – the storm is only background to the story.
  • Choice B – the barista lent her own umbrella; nothing is sold.
  • Choice D – the coffee is praised in a separate clause; the story is about the staff.
