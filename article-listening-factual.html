Factual Questions

A Factual question asks about something the speaker says directly: a reason, a time, a place, a requirement, an example, a definition. The information is in the recording. The difficulty is that it goes by only once, and the correct option says it in different words.

What it asksA specific piece of information that is clearly stated
Where it appearsListen to a Conversation · Listen to an Announcement · Listen to an Academic Talk
How oftenOne of the most common question types; often 1–2 questions in every set
Main skillCatching and remembering key details, and recognizing them in paraphrase

How to Recognize It

  • According to the speaker, …?
  • According to the announcement, …?
  • What does the woman say about …?
  • What is one reason … ?
  • Why is the man late?
  • Where will the event take place?
  • What are students asked to do?
  • What does the speaker say causes …?
  • What can attendees do besides …?
  • How do the other bees receive the information?

The phrase "according to" is the clearest signal. Plain wh-questions (why, where, when, how) about one particular point are usually factual too. Compare them with Inference questions, which use imply, suggest, infer, probably, most likely.

Which Details Get Asked

Not every detail is equally likely to be tested. Questions go to details that matter for the message. When you hear one of these cues, write the detail down:

DetailCues in the audioQuestion
Reasonsbecause, since, due to, that's why, soWhy was the event moved?
Requirements and requestsyou need to, make sure, please, don't forget, you have toWhat must students do to…?
Changesinstead, now, moved, no longer, startingWhere will the class meet?
Definitionsis called, means, refers to, in other wordsWhat is a loss leader?
How something workshere's how, first, then, what happens isHow do some mangroves remove salt?
Characteristicstend to, usually, share, featuresWhat do earworm songs have in common?
Results and effectsas a result, leads to, so, which meansWhat happens when seawater reaches the cracks?
Numbers, times, placesEspecially when two are mentioned and one replaces the otherWhen will the race begin?

Stated, But in Other Words

The correct answer almost never repeats the speaker's sentence. Expect one of these changes:

Type of changeYou hearCorrect option
Synonymregister on the websitesign up online
General wordgogglessafety equipment
Different grammarthe midterm was moved up a weekthe exam will be earlier than planned
Negative ↔ positiveit doesn't cost anythingit is free
Summary of several wordscars, factories, air conditioners pushing hot air outheat produced by human activity

The reverse is also true: an option that repeats the exact words from the audio is suspicious. It often takes a real phrase and attaches it to the wrong fact.

Factual or Inference?

Factual

The answer is said, in some form.

"The team took second place." → What place did the team take?

Inference

The answer follows from what is said.

"Their best result in over ten years." → What can be inferred? It hadn't done this well for a long time.

For a Factual question, don't go further than the speaker. If you need to "think out" the answer through several steps, you may be over-interpreting, or you may have picked an option that only sounds logical. See Inference Questions for the other side.

How Wrong Options Are Built

TrapHow it works
The neighbor detailA real detail from the recording, but it answers a different question (the start time instead of the deadline).
DistortionHalf right: the correct topic with a changed fact. Food made by the cooking club becomes a cooking class.
Old informationThe original plan, place or time, which the speaker says has changed.
Wrong personTrue about the other speaker, or about another group (volunteers vs. everyone).
ReversedCause and effect or the two sides of a comparison are swapped.
Plausible but not saidSomething that could easily be true in that situation, but no one mentions it.
ExaggeratedSome becomes all, recommended becomes required, less effective becomes doesn't work.

Strategies for Factual Questions

  • Note details with their label. Not just "Thu 3" but "NEW: Thu 3"; not just "eleven" but "volunteers: 11". A number without a label is easy to put in the wrong place.
  • Focus on what the question asks. Why? When? Who? Many wrong options are true, but they answer a different question.
  • Match meaning, not words. Translate each option back into "what the speaker would have said" and check it against your notes.
  • Check every word of the option. One wrong word (all, famous, before) makes the whole option wrong, even if the rest matches.
  • Use only the recording. If an option is true in real life but wasn't said, it is wrong here.
Example

You hear:

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a biology class.

Honeybees have a remarkable way of sharing information. When a worker bee finds a good source of food, she flies back to the hive and performs what's known as the waggle dance. She runs in a straight line while shaking her body from side to side, then circles back and repeats. The direction of that straight run tells the other bees which way to fly, measured as an angle from the sun. And the length of the waggle, how long she keeps shaking, tells them how far away the food is. The longer the waggle, the farther the food. What's surprising is that the dance happens in the dark, inside the hive. So the other bees don't actually watch it. They follow the dancer closely and sense her movements through touch and vibration.

1. According to the speaker, what does the length of the waggle communicate?

AThe amount of food available
BThe distance to the food
CThe position of the sun
DThe number of bees that are needed

2. How do the other bees receive the information?

ABy watching the dance at the entrance of the hive
BBy smelling the food on the dancer
CBy feeling the dancer's movements
DBy following the dancer to the food
Answers + Explanation

Correct Answers: 1 – B, 2 – C

Explanation

Question 1. The speaker says the length of the waggle tells the bees how far away the food is. How far = distance, so B.

  • C is the neighbor detail: the sun is used for the direction, which is communicated by the straight run, not by the length of the waggle.
  • A and D are plausible but not said: they sound like useful information for bees, but the speaker never mentions them.

Question 2. The last sentence: the bees sense her movements through touch and vibration. C paraphrases this.

  • A is directly contradicted: the dance happens in the dark, so they don't watch it.
  • D is a distortion: they follow the dancer, but only inside the hive, not to the food.
  • B is not mentioned.

Practice: 8 Factual Questions

A conversation, an announcement and an academic talk. Each recording plays only once, so take notes with labels: reasons, requirements, changes, numbers.

1 of 3 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation at a campus bookstore.

Man: Hi, I'd like to return this chemistry textbook. I rented it for the semester, but I dropped the course.

Woman: Sure. Do you have your receipt?

Man: It's on my phone. Here.

Woman: OK. Since you're returning it within the first three weeks, you'll get a full refund. After that, it's only half.

Man: Great. Will the money go back on my card?

Woman: It'll go to your student account, actually. You can use it for anything at the bookstore, or ask the finance office to transfer it to your bank.

1. Why does the man return the textbook?

AHe rented the wrong edition.
BHe is no longer taking the course.
CHe found a cheaper copy online.
DThe book was damaged.
Show answer

B. He says he dropped the course. "No longer taking the course" is the paraphrase: B. The other reasons are plausible for returning a book, but none of them is said.

2. According to the woman, where will the man's refund go?

ABack to his credit card
BTo the finance office
CTo him in cash
DTo his student account
Show answer

D. "It'll go to your student account, actually." D. A is what the man expected, and the woman corrects him. B is a distortion: the finance office can transfer the money later, but the refund doesn't go there. C is never mentioned.

2 of 3 · Announcement

Narrator: Listen to an announcement at a student club meeting.

Hello, everyone. The Environmental Club is organizing a tree-planting day this Sunday at Riverside Park, and we need volunteers. We'll meet at the park's north entrance at nine a.m. and work until around one. You don't need any experience, and we'll provide gloves and all the tools. Just wear clothes you don't mind getting dirty, and bring a water bottle. Lunch will be provided afterward by a local bakery. To sign up, send an email to the club by Friday, so we know how many trees to order.

1. What should volunteers bring?

AA water bottle
BGardening gloves
CTheir own tools
DA packed lunch
Show answer

A. "Bring a water bottle" is the only thing volunteers are asked to bring. B and C are things the club provides. D: lunch will be provided by a bakery.

2. Why does the club ask volunteers to sign up by Friday?

ATo reserve the park for Sunday
BTo order enough lunches
CTo know how many trees to order
DTo arrange transportation to the park
Show answer

C. "Send an email by Friday, so we know how many trees to order." C gives this reason. B is the neighbor detail: lunch is mentioned, but not as the reason for the deadline. A and D are plausible but never said.

3 of 3 · Academic talk

Narrator: Listen to a talk in an environmental science class.

Mangroves are trees that grow along tropical coastlines, in salty water where most plants would die. So how do they survive? Different species use different methods. Some mangroves filter salt out at their roots, so the water that reaches the rest of the tree is almost fresh. Others take the salt in but push it out through special glands on their leaves. If you lick one of those leaves, and I'm not recommending it, it tastes salty. Mangroves also have a problem with oxygen. The mud they grow in has very little, so many species send up roots that stick out above the mud, like snorkels, to take in air at low tide. These trees are incredibly valuable. Their tangled roots slow down waves and protect the coast during storms. And they're nurseries for young fish, which hide among the roots from larger predators. Unfortunately, about a third of the world's mangroves have disappeared in the last fifty years, mostly cleared for fish farms and coastal development.

1. According to the speaker, how do some mangroves get rid of salt?

ABy storing it deep in the mud
BThrough special glands on their leaves
CBy growing only where the water is fresh
DThrough roots that stick out above the mud
Show answer

B. Some species "push it out through special glands on their leaves". B. D is the neighbor detail: the roots above the mud are for oxygen, not salt. C contradicts the talk: mangroves grow in salty water. A is not said.

2. Why do some mangroves have roots that stick out above the mud?

ATo take in air
BTo catch small fish
CTo filter out salt
DTo support the tree in storms
Show answer

A. The mud has little oxygen, so these roots take in air at low tide. A. C belongs to a different group of roots. D mixes up a detail: the roots protect the coast during storms. B is not said.

3. What does the speaker say about young fish?

AThey feed on mangrove leaves.
BThey damage mangrove roots.
CThey leave the mangroves at low tide.
DThey use the roots as a hiding place.
Show answer

D. Young fish "hide among the roots from larger predators". D. The other options connect fish to mangroves in ways the speaker never mentions.

4. According to the speaker, what has caused most of the loss of mangroves?

AStrong storms along the coast
BRising levels of salt in the water
CClearing land for fish farms and building
DLarge numbers of predators
Show answer

C. Mangroves were "mostly cleared for fish farms and coastal development". Development = building, so C. A reverses the relationship: mangroves protect the coast from storms. B and D misuse words from the talk.
