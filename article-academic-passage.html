Read an Academic Passage

Read an Academic Passage tasks give you a short explanatory text, like a page from an introductory textbook, followed by five multiple-choice questions. The passages are short, but they are written in academic English, and their questions go beyond facts: you will be asked about word meaning, what the author implies, why something is mentioned and how the ideas fit together.

Where you meet it

Module 1 contains academic-passage questions worth 5 points. In the Official Guide's practice tests, Module 1 actually had two passages, one of which was an unscored tryout, and you cannot tell which one. If your Module 1 result sends you to the upper second module, you get another passage with 5 questions. The lower module has no academic passages. So academic passages are worth 10 of your 35 points on the upper path and 5 on the lower path.

This is also the only task whose level range reaches C2, which makes these passages the main way the test separates strong readers at the top of the scale.

What the passages are like

  • Expository: they explain a topic. They do not tell a story, and they rarely argue strongly for one side, although they may present different views.
  • Subjects: history, art and music, business and economics, life sciences, physical sciences and social sciences.
  • No background knowledge needed. Everything you need is in the text. If a specialized term is necessary, the passage defines it.
  • A title and one to four paragraphs. In the Official Guide's examples, passages had between two and four paragraphs.
  • Real academic difficulty. ETS describes medium-difficulty Reading texts as roughly grade 10–11 level and harder ones as grade 11–12 and above.

Most passages follow one of a few familiar patterns, and recognizing the pattern makes many questions easier:

PatternTypical signalsExample topic
General idea → examplesfor example, such as, one case isHow animals use tools
Cause → effectbecause, as a result, therefore, led toHow the printing press spread literacy
Comparison / contrastunlike, whereas, however, similarlyTwo theories of how the Moon formed
Historical developmentdates, at first, later, by the 1900s, todayThe history of paper money
Problem → solutionthe challenge, one solution, to address thisReducing noise in cities
Belief → new evidenceit was long thought, recent studies show, in factWhat dinosaurs really looked like

What the questions test

ETS lists nine skills for this task. They fall into three groups:

GroupSkills
Understanding what is statedIdentifying the main idea and basic context; understanding important details
Understanding the languageAcademic vocabulary; figurative and idiomatic expressions; the grammar academic writers use; ideas expressed in complex sentences
Seeing connectionsInferring what is not stated; relationships between ideas across sentences and paragraphs; the rhetorical structure of part or all of the text

Two patterns stood out. A Vocabulary question came with 9 of the 12 passages. And whenever there was an Insert Text question (8 passages), it was always the last question for that passage. This makes sense: by the time you reach it, you have already read the passage closely.

How it looks on the screen

The passage, with its title, sits in a scrolling panel on the left. Each question appears on its own screen on the right. Some question types change the passage panel itself: a sentence may be highlighted for Sentence Simplification, a word highlighted for Reference, the sentences of a paragraph may become clickable for Select the Sentence, or black squares may appear for Insert Text.

Strategies for Read an Academic Passage

Step 1: Map the passage first.

Spend about a minute on the title and the first sentence of each paragraph. Build a short mental map: P1 = what beavers do, P2 = effects on other species, P3 = history and today. Many questions name a paragraph, and the map tells you where to look.

Step 2: Find the exact place for each question.

Use the paragraph number or the key word in the question to locate the relevant sentences. Then read them closely, together with the sentence before and the sentence after.

Step 3: Answer in your own words first.

Before reading the options, say the answer to yourself. Then choose the option closest to it. This protects you from options that only sound right.

Step 4: Watch the signal words.

However, for example, as a result, in contrast, still show how ideas are connected. They are often the key to Rhetorical Purpose, Inference and Insert Text questions.

Step 5: Eliminate actively.

Cross out options that are not mentioned, that contradict the text, that are too extreme (always, never, only, all) or that are true but answer a different question.

Step 6: Watch the clock.

About 5 minutes per passage is a good pace. If one question is blocking you, choose your best answer and come back through Review if time allows.

Common Traps

  • True but irrelevant. The option states something the passage does say, but it does not answer this question.
  • Wrong part of the passage. The detail is real but comes from another paragraph or describes something else.
  • Too extreme. The passage says some or often; the option says all or always.
  • Reversed relationship. Cause and effect are swapped, or a contrast is turned into an agreement.
  • Outside knowledge. The option is true in the real world, but the passage does not support it.
Example: a full passage with 5 questions

Beavers as Ecosystem Engineers

Few animals change their surroundings as dramatically as the beaver. By cutting down trees and piling branches, stones, and mud across a stream, beavers build dams that slow the water and turn part of the stream into a pond. The entrance to a beaver's home, called a lodge, lies below the surface of this pond, which makes it hard for predators to reach. Because they reshape whole landscapes in this way, beavers are often described as "ecosystem engineers."

The effects of a dam reach far beyond the beavers themselves. [A] Behind the dam, the stream spreads out into a wetland that keeps water in the soil long after the rain has stopped. [B] Insects, frogs, fish, and birds move into these new habitats, and the number of species along the stream often rises. [C] The slower water also allows mud and some pollutants to settle to the bottom instead of being carried downstream. [D]

By the early twentieth century, hunting for fur had removed beavers from much of their former range in North America and Europe. Today, many regions are bringing them back. Some farmers worry that dams will flood their fields, so wildlife managers sometimes install pipes through dams to control the water level. Still, a growing number of land managers see the beaver as an inexpensive partner in restoring damaged rivers.

1. The word "dramatically" in paragraph 1 is closest in meaning to

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

Vocabulary. The paragraph goes on to describe how beavers turn a stream into a pond and "reshape whole landscapes." That is a very large, noticeable change, so dramatically means strikingly here.

  • Choice A – "gradually" means slowly and in small steps, the opposite idea.
  • Choice C – nothing in the paragraph suggests the change is hidden.
  • Choice D – the paragraph does not present the changes as dangerous; it calls beavers "engineers."

2. According to paragraph 1, why is the pond important for beavers?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

Factual. The third sentence says the lodge entrance is below the surface of the pond, "which makes it hard for predators to reach." Option B says the same thing in different words.

  • Choice A – food is not mentioned in paragraph 1.
  • Choice C – water temperature is not mentioned.
  • Choice D – travel is not mentioned.

3. Why does the author mention "insects, frogs, fish, and birds" in paragraph 2?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

Rhetorical Purpose. The paragraph opens with its main point: the effects of a dam "reach far beyond the beavers themselves." The list of animals that move into the new habitats is an example of that point.

  • Choice A – beavers are not described as hunting anything.
  • Choice C – pollution is discussed in a different sentence, and the wetland reduces it.
  • Choice D – nothing in the paragraph suggests danger to beavers.

4. What can be inferred from paragraph 3 about the pipes that wildlife managers install?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

Inference. The text says farmers worry about flooding and that managers install pipes "to control the water level." It does not say what happens to the dams, but if the pipes control the water level, the dam can stay where it is. B is the only option that follows from the stated facts.

  • Choice A – the pipes go through dams; nothing is said about catching beavers.
  • Choice C – lowering the water level does not make dams stronger.
  • Choice D – the text says managers install them; who designed them is not mentioned.

5. Look at the four letters [A], [B], [C] and [D] in paragraph 2. Where would the following sentence best fit?
As a result, these areas can stay green even during dry summers.

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

Insert Text. The new sentence starts with As a result, so it needs a cause just before it, and these areas needs a place to refer to. The sentence before [B] gives both: the wetland "keeps water in the soil long after the rain has stopped." Water in the soil is the reason the area stays green.

  • Choice A – "these areas" would have nothing to refer to, and the topic sentence gives no cause.
  • Choice C – more species do not explain why the land stays green.
  • Choice D – mud settling to the bottom is not a reason for green land in summer.

Notice that the five questions above are five different types. Expect a similar mix on the test: usually at least one question about a detail, one about vocabulary, and one or two that ask about connections, purpose or structure.
