Attitude Questions

An Attitude question asks how the speaker feels about something, what they think of it, or how sure they are. The answer lives not only in the words, but in how they are said: the tone of voice, the choice of adjectives, small words like unfortunately, honestly or maybe.

What it asksThe speaker's feelings, opinion, or level of certainty
Where it appearsListen to a Conversation · Listen to an Announcement · Listen to an Academic Talk
Main skillHearing tone and evaluative language, and not confusing the topic with the attitude

How to Recognize It

  • What is the speaker's attitude toward …?
  • How does the professor feel about …?
  • What does the speaker think about …?
  • What is the woman's opinion of …?
  • How certain is the speaker that …?
  • What is the man's attitude when he says, "…"?

Three Things Attitude Questions Ask About

1 · Opinion: positive or negative?

What is the speaker's attitude toward car-free city centers?

critical, opposedskeptical, concernedneutral, mixedsupportive, positiveenthusiastic, impressed

2 · Feelings: what emotion?

How does the woman feel after talking to the professor?

relieved encouraged amused excited surprised indifferent disappointed frustrated worried annoyed

3 · Certainty: how sure?

How certain is the speaker that T. rex had feathers?

we just don't knowmaybe, possiblyprobably, likelyclearly, no doubt

Where Attitude Shows

ClueExamplesSuggests
Evaluative wordsimpressive, remarkable, wonderful · unfortunately, a serious concern, disappointingOpinion and feelings directly
Hedgesmaybe, might, it's possible, some researchers think, I'm not sureLow certainty or caution
Boostersclearly, definitely, there's no doubt, it's hard to argue that…High certainty
Personal commentsWhat I find fascinating…, Honestly…, I have to admit…The speaker's own view (often the tested one)
"But" and "still"It's not perfect… Still, overall…What comes after the contrast carries more weight
Tone of voiceEnthusiasm, a sigh, flat delivery, laughterFeelings the words may hide or reverse
Sarcasm"Oh, fantastic." after bad newsThe opposite of the literal words

Balanced does not mean neutral

Lecturers often present both sides: benefits and drawbacks. That doesn't make their attitude "neutral". Listen for where they end up: "Still, when you look at the evidence overall…". The typical correct answer is something like generally positive, but aware of the problems.

How Wrong Options Are Built

TrapHow it works
Right topic, wrong toneDescribes the subject correctly but gets the feeling wrong: frustrated instead of concerned, skeptical instead of cautious.
A part as the wholeTakes one drawback the speaker mentioned and presents it as their overall view.
Too strongfurious, completely convinced, sees no problems when the speaker was mildly annoyed, fairly sure, or aware of limits.
Someone else's viewThe attitude of critics, "some people", or the other speaker, not of the person asked about.
Literal sarcasmTakes "Oh, great" or "fantastic" at face value.
"No opinion"Neutral, indifferent for a speaker who clearly signals a view.

Strategies for Attitude Questions

  • Put attitude into your notes. A quick +, −, ? or ! next to a point is enough: "T. rex feathers ?", "mobility problem − serious".
  • Listen for the speaker's personal voice. "What I find…", "Honestly…", "I'd say…" are where the speaker steps out of the facts and shows their view.
  • Weigh the ending. After a but, still or overall, the speaker shows where they stand.
  • Match the strength. Choose the option whose intensity fits: somewhat, generally, cautiously for moderate speakers; strongly only if the speaker was strong.
  • Check whose attitude. The speaker's, the other speaker's, or "critics"? Many wrong options describe someone else's view accurately.
  • Trust the situation over the words. If the words are positive but the situation is bad, it's probably sarcasm.
Example

You hear:

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a paleontology class.

For a long time, dinosaurs were pictured as giant scaly lizards. Today we know that picture is, at least in part, simply wrong. Since the nineteen nineties, dozens of fossils from China have been found with clear impressions of feathers. There's really no doubt anymore: many dinosaurs, especially smaller meat-eaters, had feathers. Now, what about Tyrannosaurus rex? Here, I have to be more careful. A close relative of T. rex, called Yutyrannus, definitely had feathers, which is exciting. But the few skin impressions we have from T. rex itself show scales. So maybe young T. rexes had some feathers and lost them as adults, or maybe only certain parts of the body were covered. Honestly, we just don't know yet. What I find wonderful is that the question is still open. The next fossil might answer it.

1. How certain is the speaker that many dinosaurs had feathers?

AShe is very certain because of clear fossil evidence.
BShe doubts it because the evidence comes from one country.
CShe thinks only T. rex is likely to have had feathers.
DShe believes the question cannot be answered.

2. What is the speaker's attitude toward the question of whether T. rex had feathers?

AShe is frustrated that scientists have not solved it.
BShe is unsure of the answer but excited that it is still open.
CShe thinks the question is not very important.
DShe is confident that T. rex had no feathers.
Answers + Explanation

Correct Answers: 1 – A, 2 – B

Explanation

Question 1 (certainty). Boosters: "simply wrong", "clear impressions", "There's really no doubt anymore". She is sure, and she says why. A.

  • D is a part as the whole: "we just don't know" refers only to T. rex.
  • C reverses the talk: T. rex is exactly the uncertain case.
  • B: China is where the fossils were found, but she never doubts the evidence.

Question 2 (opinion and feeling). Hedges show uncertainty: "I have to be more careful", "maybe… or maybe…", "we just don't know yet". Evaluative words show her feeling: "exciting", "What I find wonderful is that the question is still open." B has both parts.

  • A is right topic, wrong tone: she finds the open question wonderful, not frustrating.
  • D is too strong: scales on T. rex's skin make her careful, not confident.
  • C contradicts her obvious interest.

Practice: 8 Attitude Questions

Two conversations, an academic talk and an announcement. Each recording plays only once. Mark attitude in your notes with + − ? !

The audio uses your browser's built-in English voices.

Note: browser voices can't always show emotion or sarcasm the way a real speaker would. In these tasks, rely more on the words and the situation. On the real test, the voice will help you more.

1 of 4 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between two students.

Woman: Did you hear the library's going to stay open twenty-four hours during exam weeks?

Man: Yeah. I'm not sure how I feel about it, to be honest.

Woman: Really? I thought you'd love it. You're always there until closing.

Man: That's exactly the problem. If it never closes, I'll never go home. I'll be the guy sleeping on the third floor.

Woman: Ha! Well, I think it's great. My roommate's loud, and I can never study at home.

1. What is the man's attitude toward the new library hours?

AHe is strongly against them.
BHe is very excited about them.
CHe has mixed feelings about them.
DHe does not care about them.
Show answer

C. "I'm not sure how I feel about it" and then a joke about never going home: he sees the use but also a risk for himself. C. A is too strong: he only jokes about a problem. B is what the woman expected, not what he feels. D: he clearly has a reaction.

2. How does the woman feel about the change?

AShe welcomes it.
BShe is worried about safety at night.
CShe thinks it is unnecessary.
DShe is surprised that the man likes it.
Show answer

A. "I think it's great", because she can't study at home. A. D reverses the situation: she is surprised that he doesn't love it. B and C are not supported.

2 of 4 · Academic talk

Narrator: Listen to a talk in an urban planning class.

Over the past twenty years, a number of European cities have closed parts of their centers to cars. The results have been, in many ways, impressive. Air quality has improved, streets are quieter, and in several cities, shops in car-free areas actually report more customers, not fewer. People walk more and stay longer. Now, I don't want to pretend it's a perfect solution. Delivery companies often struggle, and people with limited mobility can find it harder to reach the center, which is a serious concern that planners haven't fully solved. And a policy that works in a compact medieval city won't necessarily work in a spread-out city built around highways. Still, when you look at the evidence overall, it's hard to argue that these experiments have failed. If anything, I'd say more cities should be trying them, carefully, and with the problems I just mentioned in mind.

1. What is the speaker's overall attitude toward car-free city centers?

AHe is enthusiastic and sees no real problems.
BHe is generally positive but aware of their limitations.
CHe is mostly critical of them.
DHe has no clear opinion about them.
Show answer

B. "Impressive" results, then honest drawbacks, then "Still… more cities should be trying them, carefully". The ending shows where he stands. B. A is too strong: he names several problems. C takes the drawbacks as the whole view. D: balanced is not neutral.

2. How does the speaker feel about the difficulties faced by people with limited mobility?

AHe thinks the problem is exaggerated.
BHe believes planners have already solved it.
CHe considers it less important than air quality.
DHe sees it as a serious issue that still needs solutions.
Show answer

D. "A serious concern that planners haven't fully solved". D. B contradicts "haven't fully solved". A is the opposite of "serious". C is never compared.

3. How certain is the speaker that car-free centers would work in every city?

AHe is not sure, because it depends on how a city is built.
BHe is completely certain that they would.
CHe is sure they would fail outside Europe.
DHe believes they only work in small towns.
Show answer

A. "Won't necessarily work in a spread-out city built around highways" is a hedge about some cities. A. B ignores that hedge. C and D are too strong: he talks about city design, not about Europe or town size.

3 of 4 · Announcement

Narrator: Listen to an announcement at a university concert hall.

Hello, everyone. I'm sorry to say that tonight's concert by the university orchestra has been cancelled. The conductor is ill, and we simply couldn't find a replacement at such short notice. I know many of you have been looking forward to this for weeks, and so were we. The orchestra has worked incredibly hard this semester. We're doing our best to reschedule for early next month, and all tickets will remain valid for the new date.

What is the speaker's attitude in the announcement?

AShe is angry with the conductor.
BShe is relieved that the concert was cancelled.
CShe is disappointed but hopeful about a new date.
DShe is indifferent to the cancellation.
Show answer

C. "I'm sorry to say", "so were we" and praise for the orchestra show disappointment. "We're doing our best to reschedule" adds hope. C. A: the conductor is ill, and she doesn't blame him. B and D contradict her tone.

4 of 4 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between two students.

Man: How was the group meeting?

Woman: Oh, fantastic. Two people didn't show up, and the one who did hadn't read the assignment.

Man: Ouch. So you're doing most of the work again?

Woman: Looks like it. But you know what, I talked to the professor after class, and she's going to let us evaluate each other at the end.

Man: That's something, at least.

Woman: Yeah. For the first time this semester, I feel like somebody's listening.

1. What is the woman's attitude when she says, "Oh, fantastic"?

AShe is genuinely pleased with the meeting.
BShe is surprised that the meeting was so short.
CShe is excited about the assignment.
DShe is being sarcastic: the meeting went badly.
Show answer

D. Positive words, bad situation: two people didn't come, and the third wasn't prepared. That's sarcasm. D. A is the literal-sarcasm trap. B and C are not supported.

2. How does the woman feel after talking to the professor?

AShe still feels hopeless.
BShe feels somewhat encouraged.
CShe is annoyed with the professor.
DShe is confident she will get the best grade.
Show answer

B. "For the first time this semester, I feel like somebody's listening" is a positive but modest feeling, and the man agrees it's "something, at least". B. D is too strong: grades aren't mentioned. A and C are the opposite of her final lines.

Recognize

attitude toward, feel about, think about, opinion of, how certain

Listen for

Evaluative words, hedges and boosters, personal comments, what comes after but / still, tone and sarcasm.

Avoid

Wrong tone · a part as the whole · too strong · someone else's view · literal sarcasm · "no opinion".
