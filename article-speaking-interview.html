Take an Interview

In this task, you take part in a short online interview with a recorded interviewer, for example as a volunteer in a university research study or as an applicant for a scholarship. The interviewer asks 4 questions on one topic, and you have 45 seconds to answer each one, with no time to prepare.

This is the part of the Speaking section where you use your own words: you describe your experiences, explain your opinions and support them. It's scored on what you say and how you say it.

What you doAnswer an interviewer's questions in a simulated conversation
Number of questions4, all on one topic, from personal to abstract
Time to speak45 seconds per question; the timer starts as soon as the question ends
Preparation timeNone
PositionThe second and last task of the Speaking section (questions 8–11)
Scored onRelevance · elaboration · delivery · language use
Points0–5 per answer, 20 in total
LevelA1–C2

Memorized answers are penalized

ETS states that responses which seem to contain memorized language receive a lower score. Useful phrases for linking and giving examples are fine; reciting a prepared answer or a template is not. Practice speaking, not scripts.

How It Works on the Screen

  1. Introduction. A narrator reads the situation aloud, and you see a photo of the interviewer. For example: You have volunteered for a research study about how students manage their time. You will have a short online interview with a researcher.
  2. Question 1 starts immediately. The interviewer appears on video and asks the question.
  3. Your 45 seconds begin right away. There's no beep and no pause. A timer shows the remaining time, and the interviewer nods as if listening.
  4. When time is up, your response is saved and the next question starts automatically.

How the Interviewer Asks

Each question usually has three parts. Only the last one is the actual question, so keep listening until the end:

Reaction

"Interesting, thank you."

Context

"Some people say that students today have less free time than students twenty years ago, because of part-time jobs and phone notifications."

Question

"Do you agree? Why or why not?"

Watch for two-part questions: "What happened, and what did you do?", "Which do you prefer, and why?" A relevant answer covers both parts. The context part is also useful: it often offers two sides you can react to.

From Personal to Abstract: the 4 Questions

The questions grow in difficulty. Here is a typical interview from a research study on time management:

1

What does a typical weekday look like for you?

Personal, factual. Describe your routine. Easy to talk about, but still needs details.

2

Tell me about a time when distractions stopped you from finishing something important. What happened, and what did you do?

Personal experience. Tell a short story in the past tense, both parts.

3

Some people say students today have less free time than twenty years ago. Do you agree?

Opinion on a trend. Take a position and support it.

4

Imagine your university banned phones in all classrooms. Would that help students manage their time better?

Hypothetical. Imagine consequences and reason about them.

How Each Answer Is Scored

Each answer gets a single holistic score from 0 to 5, based on four dimensions:

Relevance

Do you answer the question that was asked, all of it?

Elaboration

Do you support your ideas with reasons, details and examples, connected clearly?

Delivery

Is your pace natural and conversational? Are your pronunciation, rhythm and intonation clear?

Language use

Do you use a range of grammar and vocabulary accurately enough to express precise meaning?

Here is the complete scoring guide, point by point, in our own words. The original wording is in The Official Guide to the TOEFL iBT® Test (2026 Pocket Edition), Chapter 5, "Take an Interview Task Scoring Guide".

Take an Interview Task Scoring Guide

Score General Description
5

A fully successful response

The response fully answers the question, and it is clear and fluent.

A typical response shows the following:

  • The response is on topic and well developed.
  • A good conversational pace is kept, with pauses that are appropriate and sound natural.
  • Pronunciation is easy to understand; rhythm and intonation help carry the meaning.
  • A range of accurate grammar and vocabulary makes it possible to express precise meanings clearly.
4

A generally successful response

The response answers the question, and it is reasonably clear.

A typical response shows the following:

  • The response is on topic and developed, but it may lack effective connectors between sentences.
  • A good pace is mostly kept; some pauses may slightly affect the flow.
  • Pronunciation, rhythm and intonation do not get in the way of meaning, although a few words or phrases may take a little effort to understand.
  • Grammar and vocabulary are good enough to express general meanings most of the time.
3

A partially successful response

The response answers the question, but its development and/or clarity is limited.

A typical response shows the following:

  • The response is mostly on topic, but it is not developed very much.
  • Frequent or long pauses make the pace choppy; filler words are frequent.
  • Intelligibility is sometimes affected by mistakes in how words are pronounced or in stress and rhythm.
  • A limited range and accuracy of grammar and vocabulary clearly limit how precisely and clearly ideas are expressed.
2

A mostly unsuccessful response

The response tries to answer the question, but it is not supported in a meaningful and/or intelligible way.

A typical response shows the following:

  • The response is only loosely connected to the question and has little or no relevant development, or it is made up mostly of words from the question.
  • Intelligibility is limited; it is often hard to tell what the speaker means.
  • The range of grammar and vocabulary is very limited.
1

An unsuccessful response

The response barely addresses the question, and it may show very limited control of the language.

A typical response shows the following:

  • The response is connected to the question only through a few of its words.
  • Most of the response cannot be understood.
  • The response consists mainly of separate words or phrases.
0

No response OR the response cannot be understood at all OR it contains no English OR its content has nothing to do with the prompt (or consists only of phrases such as “I don’t know”).

What the guide tells you

  • Between 5 and 4: connectors and precision. A 4 is developed but may lack good links between sentences; a 5 flows and expresses exact meanings.
  • Between 4 and 3: development and flow. A 3 is on topic but thin, with frequent or long pauses and many fillers.
  • Between 3 and 2: real content. A 2 barely answers the question or mostly repeats its words.
  • Fillers are allowed. Top-level sample answers in the Official Guide contain um, uh and like. Only frequent fillers with a choppy pace pull the score down.

Filling 45 Seconds

45 seconds is longer than it feels when you are speaking. A simple, flexible plan helps you use the time without memorizing anything:

Answer
Reason
Example / details
Wrap-up
0 s~6 s~18 s~36 s45 s
  1. Answer directly in the first sentence. No long introduction. "I'd say a teacher in person works better, at least for most skills."
  2. Give the main reason. "The main reason is feedback."
  3. Make it concrete with a personal example or specific details. This is the longest part and the one that makes an answer "well elaborated".
  4. Add a contrast or wrap up. "On the other hand, videos are great for quick things… So overall…" If time is left, add another detail rather than stopping.

For personal questions, the same plan becomes answer → story or details → how you felt or what you learned.

Don't lengthen answers with prepared openings or endings

The Official Guide specifically warns against memorized introductory or concluding phrases and sentences used to make a response longer. Fill the time with content: more details, another example, a reason.

Common Mistakes

MistakeWhy it costs points
Stopping after 15 secondsNot enough elaboration. The Official Guide advises using the full 45 seconds and adding details or examples if you finish early.
Answering only halfTwo-part questions need both parts for full relevance.
Repeating the question"I think students today have less free time than twenty years ago because…" uses the interviewer's words instead of yours. Mostly repeating the question is a feature of a 2.
A prepared story that doesn't fitMemorized language is penalized, and an off-topic story lowers relevance.
Listing without developing"It's cheap, it's fast, it's easy" is three reasons with no support. One reason with an example is better.
Long silences while thinkingThe scoring guide mentions long pauses as a feature of lower scores. Short pauses and some fillers are normal; long gaps are what to avoid.
RushingSpeaking very fast to say more reduces clarity. A steady conversational pace is what the guide rewards.

Strategies for Take an Interview

  • Listen to the end of the question. The actual question is the last sentence. Notice if it has two parts.
  • Start speaking immediately. The timer is already running. Your first sentence is your direct answer; you can think while saying it.
  • One idea, well developed. A reason with a real example beats a list of three reasons.
  • Talk to a person, not a microphone. It's a conversation: natural tone, small reactions, varied intonation. The interviewer nodding on screen is a reminder of that.
  • Keep going until the time ends. If you finish early, add a detail, a contrast or a short conclusion. It's fine if the timer cuts off your last sentence.
Example: two answers compared

The interviewer asks (question 3 of a study on learning new skills):

"Thank you. Now, some people prefer learning from videos online, while others prefer a teacher in person. Which do you think works better, and why?"

A strong answer (likely 5) · about 110 words

Hmm, I think it really depends on the skill, but for most things I'd say a teacher in person works better. The main reason is feedback. When I was learning guitar, I watched a lot of videos, and I thought I was doing fine. But when I finally took a lesson, the teacher noticed right away that I was holding my wrist wrong, and that's why my hand always hurt. A video can't see you, you know? On the other hand, videos are great for, um, quick things, like fixing a bike tire, because you can pause and watch again. So overall, for anything complicated, I'd choose a real teacher.

Answer Reason Example Contrast Wrap-up

Answers both parts (which + why), develops one reason with a specific story, adds a fair contrast, and connects ideas with but, and that's why, on the other hand, so overall. The hmm and um sound natural and don't break the flow.

A weak answer (likely 2–3) · about 35 words

"I think teacher in person is better. Because teacher can help you. Videos is also good, but… um… teacher is better for me. Yes. Because teacher can help. That's it."

It answers the question, but the reason is never developed and is repeated instead of explained. There's no example, the grammar is very limited (teacher without an article, videos is), and it ends after about 15 seconds, leaving 30 seconds unused.

Practice: Interview Simulation

This works like the real task: an introduction, then 4 questions asked once, and 45 seconds for each answer, starting immediately after the question. The questions are hidden until the end. Then you can listen to your answers and check them against the scoring guide.

The interactive simulator needs JavaScript. You can still practice with a partner: they read each question once, and you answer for 45 seconds with a timer.

Research study: time management: questions for your partner

You have volunteered for a research study about how university students manage their time. You will have a short online interview with a researcher. The researcher will ask you some questions.

  1. Thanks for joining me today. Let's start with something simple. What does a typical weekday look like for you?
  2. Thank you. Most people have to deal with distractions. Can you tell me about a time when you had trouble finishing something important because of distractions? What happened, and what did you do?
  3. Interesting. Some people say that students today have less free time than students did twenty years ago, because of part-time jobs and constant notifications on their phones. Do you agree? Why or why not?
  4. Good points. One last question. Imagine your university decided to ban phones in all classrooms. Do you think this would help students manage their time better? Why or why not?
Scholarship: community involvement: questions for your partner

You are applying for a scholarship that supports students who are active in their communities. You will have a short online interview with a member of the scholarship committee.

  1. Thank you for speaking with me. First, could you tell me what you are studying, or plan to study, and why you chose it?
  2. Thanks. Now tell me about the people around you. How do your friends or family members usually help others in your community?
  3. That's great to hear. Looking ahead, how do you think volunteering will change in the next ten years, for example because of technology? Why?
  4. Interesting. Finally, some people believe that universities should require every student to do volunteer work before graduating. Others think volunteering should always be a personal choice. What is your view? Please explain.
