Inference

Inference questions ask about something the text does not say directly but clearly implies. The answer is not written in the text, but it is built entirely from what is written: one or two stated facts lead to a conclusion that the author expects the reader to reach.

What the question tests

Writers often leave things unsaid because they expect readers to understand them. If an email says you no longer need to print your essays, it does not add you print them now: the reader is expected to see it. Inference questions check that you can read these implied meanings, in academic passages and in everyday texts.

Typical wording

  • What can be inferred about …?
  • The passage implies that …
  • What does the author suggest about …?
  • What is suggested about …?
  • The email is most likely intended for …
  • What will the writer most likely do next?

Words like infer, imply, suggest, likely and probably usually signal an Inference question, but not always. Sometimes the question looks like a simple Factual question, and you only realize while reading that the answer is not stated anywhere.

Strategies for Inference Questions

Step 1: Start from what is stated.

Find the part of the text the question is about and note exactly what it says. The correct inference is always built on these facts.

Step 2: Take one small step.

Ask: if this is true, what else must be true? The right answer is a short, logical step, not a long chain of guesses.

Step 3: Look for links between ideas.

Cause and effect, contrast, comparison, before and after: these relationships are where most inferences come from.

Step 4: Consider the writer's tone and purpose.

In Daily Life texts especially, the tone tells you a lot: who is writing, to whom, and what they want.

Step 5: Eliminate.

Remove options that contradict the text, that the text does not support, and that go too far. Choose the one that is strongly supported, not the one that is merely possible.

Common Traps

  • Too far. The option might be true, but nothing in the text supports it. Inference does not mean imagination.
  • Contradiction. The option goes against something the text states.
  • Extreme words. All, never, only, most people often turn a reasonable inference into an unsupported one.
  • Real-world logic. The option sounds sensible in general, but it is not based on this text.
  • The wrong detail. The option is a correct inference about something else in the text, not about what the question asks.
Example 1: combining two facts

Finding a Ship's Position

For centuries, sailors could work out their latitude, their position north or south, by measuring the height of the Sun or certain stars above the horizon. Finding longitude, their position east or west, was far harder. To calculate it, a navigator needed to compare local time on the ship with the time at a fixed home port, and that required a clock that stayed accurate for weeks on a rolling, damp ship. Such clocks were not developed until the eighteenth century.

1. What can be inferred about measuring longitude at sea before the eighteenth century?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: C

Explanation

The passage gives two facts: measuring longitude required an accurate clock, and such clocks did not exist until the eighteenth century. Put them together and the conclusion follows, even though no sentence states it: before then, longitude could not be measured accurately.

  • Choice A – it contradicts the passage: longitude was "far harder."
  • Choice B – the stars are mentioned only for latitude; longitude needed a clock.
  • Choice D – the passage says nothing about short or long voyages.
Example 2: a Daily Life notice
History Department — Important Change

Starting next semester, students will no longer need to print their essays. All assignments will be submitted through the online course portal.

The department office will stop accepting paper copies on January 15. Questions? Visit the office (Room 120) during opening hours.

1. What can be inferred from the notice?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: A

Explanation

The phrase no longer need to print only makes sense if students print their essays now. That is the kind of small, safe step an inference question expects: one logical step from what is written.

  • Choice B – nothing is said about who else uses the portal.
  • Choice C – students' preferences are not mentioned; this goes too far beyond the text.
  • Choice D – the office will stop accepting paper copies on that date, not close.
