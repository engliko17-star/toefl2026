Time Management in Reading

Reading gives you about 30 minutes for about 50 questions, roughly 36 seconds per question on average. But the time is not spread evenly: one missing letter can take a few seconds, while an academic passage needs several minutes. This guide explains how the clock works, gives you a time plan for each module and a pace calculator, and shows what to do when you fall behind.

How the clock works

These are the rules ETS describes in the Official Guide. They decide what a good time strategy looks like.

  • Each module has its own time limit: up to 21 minutes for Module 1 and up to 9 minutes for Module 2. In the Official Guide's practice tests, Module 1 allows about 20 minutes, so plan for 20 and treat the last minute as a buffer.
  • The clock counts down in the toolbar. You can hide it with Hide Time, but when only a little time is left, the clock appears with an alert and can no longer be hidden.
  • Help does not stop the clock. Learn the directions for every task before test day so you never need to open them.
  • You can move freely inside a module with Back, Next and Review. Review shows which questions you skipped and lets you jump to any of them. Once you start Module 2, you cannot return to Module 1.
  • There is no penalty for wrong answers. A blank answer and a wrong answer both earn nothing, so a guess can only help.
  • Module 1 decides your path. Your result in Module 1 determines the difficulty of Module 2. Rushing Module 1 to save time is a bad trade.

How Honeybees Cool Their Hive

A honeybee colony works hard to keep the temperature of its nest steady. The young bees developing in the brood area need about 34 to 35 degrees Celsius, and even a few degrees more can harm them. On hot days, the workers therefore act together as a kind of living air conditioner.

The first response is ventilation. Groups of bees stand near the entrance and beat their wings rapidly, pushing warm air out of the hive and drawing cooler air in. If this is not enough, foragers begin to collect water instead of nectar. Back in the hive, they spread thin films of water over the comb, and other bees fan the wet surfaces. As the water evaporates, it carries heat away, in much the same way that sweating cools the human body.

When the hive becomes crowded, many bees also leave the interior and gather in a dense cluster on the outside wall, a behavior beekeepers call "bearding." With fewer bodies inside, less heat is produced near the brood, and the remaining workers can keep the nursery at a safe temperature.

What would save you the most time in Module 1

    A rough model: one careful reading of each text plus your time per question or per missing word. Text lengths are typical lengths of real test items in our database. The limits are ETS's: up to 21 minutes for Module 1 and up to 9 for Module 2. In reality, faster reading also shortens your time per question, because you reread parts of the text to answer.

    Rules for the clock

    The first seven points follow ETS's own time-management advice in the Official Guide; the limits and examples are ours.

    1

    Pace yourself across task types.

    ETS's first time-management advice is not to spend too much time on any single task. In practice this means working to the checkpoints: Complete the Words should not eat into the time for the academic passages.

    2

    Check the clock at the start of each new text.

    ETS recommends keeping track of the time remaining and adjusting your pace. Looking at the clock after every question costs focus; looking at it at the start of each text is enough, and it matches the checkpoints above.

    3

    Read for the overall meaning first, then go back for details.

    ETS advises reading for broad understanding rather than perfect comprehension. The text stays on the screen, so you never need to remember details: read once for the main point of each paragraph, then return to the exact sentence each question is about.

    4

    Give one question one attempt.

    If you have thought about a question and still don't know, ETS suggests eliminating as many options as you can and choosing the best remaining one. A useful limit: about a minute for an academic question, half a minute for a Daily Life question. Then choose, and move on.

    5

    Never leave a question blank.

    There is no penalty for incorrect answers, so a blank is the only answer that is certainly worth zero. If you skip a question to come back later, pick your best guess first, so it is not empty if time runs out.

    6

    Use Review only at the end.

    ETS suggests using Review after all the questions have been answered. Jumping back and forth during the module costs time and focus. Finish the last question, then use the remaining minute to revisit the questions you guessed.

    7

    Don't second-guess.

    Make a decision and move forward. Changing an answer is worth it only when you have found a sentence in the text that clearly supports a different option, not because of a vague feeling.

    8

    Protect Module 1.

    Module 1 is long and decides which Module 2 you get. Do not save time there by rushing: accuracy in Module 1 is worth more than a spare minute at the end of it.

    In order, or easiest first?

    ETS suggests that if you need to work more quickly, you should answer the easier, clearly answerable questions first and skip a difficult question to return to it through Review. For most students the best way to apply this is to keep to the order of the module and skip only single hard questions, not whole texts. There are two reasons:

    • Questions usually follow the order of the text. In the real test items in our database, the next academic question referred to a later part of the passage in 92% of cases, and in Daily Life texts in 74%. Working in order means you read each part of the text once.
    • Jumping between texts means rereading them. Every return to a passage you have left costs you the reading time again.

    Skipping a whole text makes sense only at the very end of a module, when there is clearly not enough time for it. Then answer the quickest questions you can, such as a Vocabulary question, and guess the rest.

    When you fall behind

    If you reach a checkpoint late, don't panic. Change how you work for the rest of the module:

    1. More than a minute behind before the academic passages: skip the full first reading. Read the first sentence of each paragraph, then go straight to the questions and read only the part each question points to.
    2. A question is not coming: eliminate what you can, choose, and go on. Don't return to it until everything else is answered.
    3. Complete the Words is slowing you down: fill in the short, quick words first (the, and, of, such), then go back to the longer ones. Type your best guess in every gap.
    4. The last minute: stop solving and open Review. Give an answer to every question that is still blank. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, and a blank is always worth zero.

    Common Time Traps

    • Rereading the whole text for every question. Read it once for the general idea, then go to the exact place. Questions usually move through the text from top to bottom.
    • Perfectionism in Complete the Words. Ten gaps in a paragraph and one point each: one stubborn word is not worth a minute.
    • Opening Help during the test. The clock keeps running. Know all the directions before test day.
    • Hiding the clock and forgetting it. If the clock distracts you, hide it, but check it at the start of each text.
    • Leaving blanks for later. If time runs out, they stay blank. Put in a guess before you move on.
    • Racing through Module 1. Spare time at the end of Module 1 is no use in Module 2, and mistakes in Module 1 can send you to the lower module.

    How to get faster before the test

    Reading speed affects every part of the module: the first reading of each text and every time you look back at it to answer a question. This is how long one careful reading of a typical text takes at different speeds:

    Text (typical length)100 wpm150 wpm200 wpm
    Daily Life, short (~50 words)30 s20 s15 s
    Daily Life, long (~150 words)1:301:000:45
    Academic passage (~200 words)2:001:201:00

    Time for one careful reading. At 100 words per minute, simply reading an academic passage once takes 2 of the 5 minutes you have for it.

    • Read a lot of easy material. Studies of reading fluency find that speed grows with large amounts of easy, enjoyable reading. In one study of Japanese university students, those who improved most had read over 200,000 words of graded readers in a year (Beglar & Hunt, 2014).
    • Do short timed reading sessions. Read an easy text against the clock for 10–15 minutes a day and check that you still understand it. Paul Nation suggests about 250 words per minute on easy material as a goal for learners and warns that reading much slower than 100 words per minute makes comprehension harder.
    • Grow your vocabulary. Unknown words slow you down more than anything else. The fewer unknown words in a passage, the less often you stop.
    • Practise whole modules with the real limits. Do at least a few full timed modules (20 minutes, then 9) before the test, using the checkpoints. Timing is a skill you train, not something you can plan only on paper.

    For comparison, adult native speakers read non-fiction silently at about 238 words per minute on average (Brysbaert, 2019). You don't need that speed for the TOEFL: at 150 words per minute, the calculator above shows that the plan fits comfortably.
