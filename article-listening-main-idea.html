Main Idea Questions

A Main Idea question asks about the whole recording: what the speakers are mainly talking about, or why the speaker is making the announcement or giving the talk. To answer it, you need the big picture, not a single detail.

What it asksThe central topic or the overall purpose of the recording
Where it appearsListen to a Conversation · Listen to an Announcement · Listen to an Academic Talk
Main skillSeparating the central message from supporting details and examples

How to Recognize It

Main Idea questions come in two versions. They look different, but you prepare for them in the same way.

Topic: "What about?"

  • What is the conversation mainly about?
  • What are the speakers mainly discussing?
  • What is the main topic of the talk?
  • What is the announcement mainly about?
  • What is the woman's problem?

Answers are noun phrases: A change in the time of a race.

Purpose: "Why?"

  • What is the main purpose of the announcement?
  • Why does the speaker make the announcement?
  • Why does the man go to see the woman?
  • What is the speaker's main point?

Answers usually start with To + verb: To explain a new policy.

Where the Main Idea Is

The main idea is rarely hidden in the middle. Each task type has its own "place" for it:

Task typeWhere to listen
ConversationThe first 2–3 lines: the question, problem or news that starts the conversation. The rest is how the speakers react to it.
AnnouncementThe sentence right after the greeting: "…has been moved", "Starting Monday…", "Congratulations to…"
Academic talkAfter the hook, where the key term appears: "That's called…", "Today let's look at…". Then check that the rest of the talk develops it.

The main idea is not always said directly

Sometimes nobody states it in one sentence. Two students may complain about renovations in their dorm without ever saying "we're discussing the renovations". Then the main idea is what all the lines have in common. Signal phrases like "Let's focus on…" or "What I want to highlight is…" help when they appear.

The "Just Right" Test

The correct answer covers the whole recording: everything important in it fits under the answer, and the answer doesn't cover much more than that. Wrong options are usually too narrow or too broad. Here is the countershading talk from the Academic Talk guide:

Too narrow
Why sharks are dark on top — one example
Too narrow
Why countershading fails in forests — only the last part
Just right
How a color pattern helps animals avoid being seen
Too broad
How animals survive in the ocean — a whole field, not this talk
Off target
How animals change color by season — related, but never discussed

A quick check: could the option be the title of this recording? A title that fits only one paragraph, or one that would fit a whole textbook chapter, is wrong.

How Wrong Options Are Built

TrapHow it works
A detail as the topicSomething that really was said, but only once and in passing: a price, a place, a name.
The example as the topicIn talks especially: the milk ad, the sharks. Examples illustrate the main idea; they are not it.
The hook as the topicThe opening story or question. It leads into the topic but is not what the talk is about.
Too broadThe general area ("health on campus", "ocean animals") instead of the specific subject.
A familiar word in a new storyUses words you heard (tuition, budget) to describe something that was never discussed, like a "plan to raise tuition".
The wrong purposeFor "Why…?" questions: the right topic with the wrong intention, e.g. To cancel instead of To reschedule, To complain instead of To inform.

Strategies for Main Idea Questions

  • Ask "Why is this person talking?" To solve a problem, share news, explain an idea, change a plan? The answer to this question is very often the main idea.
  • Write a headline before you read the options. As soon as the audio ends, put the topic into 3–6 words in your notes. Then find the option closest to it.
  • Check the beginning against the end. The correct answer fits both the opening and the conclusion. If an option fits only one part, it's a detail.
  • Expect paraphrase. "Moved to Thursday" becomes "a new day for a workshop". Choose by meaning, not by matching words.
  • For "Why…?" versions, check the verb. Explain, remind, invite, warn, request, complain, announce: the verb in the option must match what the speaker actually does.
Example

You hear:

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between two students.

M: Did you get the email about the chemistry midterm?

W: No, what happened?

M: Professor Ruiz moved it up a week. It's next Monday now, not the Monday after.

W: You're kidding. I haven't even started reviewing. And I've got a paper due Friday.

M: Same here. A few of us are meeting in the library tomorrow night to go over the problem sets. Want to join?

W: Definitely. That's probably the only way I'll get through it.

What are the speakers mainly discussing?

AA problem with an email from their professor
BA paper the woman has to finish by Friday
CAn exam that will take place earlier than planned
DHow to reserve a study room in the library
Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: C

Explanation

The news in line 3 starts everything: the midterm was moved up a week. Everything after that is a reaction to it: the woman hasn't started reviewing, and the man suggests a study group. Moved up = earlier than planned, so C is correct. Headline test: "Chem midterm moved earlier" covers all six lines.

  • A uses a familiar word in a new story: the email only brings the news; nothing is wrong with it.
  • B is a detail as the topic: the paper is mentioned once, to show how busy she is.
  • D is off target: they will meet in the library, but reserving a room is never discussed.

Practice: 6 Main Idea Questions

Two conversations, two announcements and two short talks, one Main Idea question for each. Every recording plays only once. When it ends, write a 3–6 word headline in your notes before you look at the options.

1 of 6 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between two students.

Woman: Hey, do you know why the laundry room is locked?

Man: There was a sign this morning. They're replacing all the washing machines.

Woman: All of them? Mine was working fine last week.

Man: Apparently the new ones use half the water, and you can pay with your phone instead of coins.

Woman: Well, no more hunting for quarters, I guess. How long is it closed?

Man: Until Thursday. In the meantime, we can use the one in Baker Hall.

What is the conversation mainly about?

AHow to pay for laundry with a phone
BA problem with the woman's washing machine
CWhy the laundry room in their building is temporarily closed
DWays to save water on campus
Show answer

C. The first line asks why the laundry room is locked, and everything after explains the reason (new machines) and how long it will last. C covers the whole conversation. A and D are details about the new machines, too narrow. B misreads the woman's line: her machine was working fine.

2 of 6 · Conversation

Narrator: Listen to a conversation between a student and an academic adviser.

Man: Hi, Ms. Chen. I wanted to ask about dropping my statistics class.

Woman: OK. Is something wrong?

Man: I just started a part-time job, and the class meets at the same time as my Tuesday shift.

Woman: I see. Well, the deadline to drop without a penalty was last Friday. But there's an evening section of the same course with a few seats left.

Man: Oh, so I wouldn't lose the credits?

Woman: Exactly. You'd just switch sections. Let me check if you can still register for it.

Why does the man go to see the woman?

ATo apply for a part-time job at the college
BTo ask for more time to finish an assignment
CTo complain about a penalty he received
DTo solve a conflict between his class and his work schedule
Show answer

D. He wants to drop the class because it clashes with his new job, and the adviser offers a way to keep the course. D describes the purpose of the visit. A: he already has a job. C misuses "penalty": the adviser only mentions the deadline, and he hasn't received any penalty. B is never mentioned.

3 of 6 · Announcement

Narrator: Listen to an announcement from a university dining service.

Good morning. This is a message from Campus Dining. As part of our effort to reduce waste, starting next week, all dining halls will stop providing disposable cups and plastic utensils. Instead, every student will receive a free reusable bottle and a set of metal utensils at the dining hall entrance. You can pick yours up any day next week with your student card. And if you bring your own cup to any campus café, you'll get ten percent off your drink.

What is the main purpose of the announcement?

ATo introduce a new menu in the dining halls
BTo explain a change designed to reduce waste
CTo advertise a discount at campus cafés
DTo remind students to carry their student cards
Show answer

B. The key sentence follows the greeting: to reduce waste, dining halls will stop giving out disposable items and will hand out reusable ones instead. B states this purpose. C and D are details from the end of the announcement. A is never mentioned.

4 of 6 · Announcement

Narrator: Listen to an announcement at a sports event.

Attention, runners. Due to strong winds this morning, the start of the charity five K race has been delayed by thirty minutes. The race will now begin at ten thirty instead of ten. The course hasn't changed, and all water stations will be open as planned. If you haven't picked up your race number yet, the registration tent near the main gate is still open. Thanks for your patience, and remember, every step today helps raise money for the children's hospital.

What is the announcement mainly about?

AA change in the starting time of a race
BThe route of a charity race
CHow the money from a race will be used
DWhere runners can collect their race numbers
Show answer

A. The first real sentence gives the news: the start is delayed by thirty minutes. The rest confirms what hasn't changed and adds small reminders. A is correct. B: the speaker only says the course hasn't changed. C and D are details from the end.

5 of 6 · Academic talk

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a history class.

Many ancient Roman structures, like harbors built into the sea, are still standing after two thousand years. Modern concrete, in contrast, often starts to crack within a few decades. So what did the Romans know that we don't? For a long time, scientists assumed Roman concrete was simply made more carefully. But recent studies suggest the secret is in the ingredients. The Romans mixed lime with volcanic ash. When seawater gets into cracks in this material, it reacts with the ash and forms new minerals. Those minerals actually fill the cracks, so the concrete gets stronger over time instead of weaker. Modern concrete, on the other hand, is designed to stay the same once it's hard, and seawater slowly damages it. Now, engineers are trying to copy the Roman recipe. It won't replace modern concrete everywhere, because it hardens much more slowly, but for structures like sea walls, it could be a real improvement.

What is the talk mainly about?

AThe history of Roman harbors
BHow volcanoes produce new minerals
CThe dangers of building near the sea
DWhy ancient Roman concrete has lasted so long
Show answer

D. The talk asks what the Romans knew, explains the volcanic ash and self-healing cracks, and ends with engineers copying the recipe. D covers it all. A is the hook: harbors are just the opening example. B takes words from the explanation (volcanic, minerals) and builds a different topic. C is too broad and not the focus.

6 of 6 · Academic talk

Narrator: Listen to a talk in a psychology class.

You know that feeling when a song gets stuck in your head and just won't leave? Psychologists call this an earworm, and almost everyone experiences them. Researchers have found that earworms usually share a few features. The songs tend to have a fast tempo, a simple melody, and some unusual element, like an unexpected jump in pitch, that makes them stand out. But why does the brain keep replaying them? One theory is that it's related to unfinished tasks. When we hear only part of a song, the brain treats it as incomplete and keeps returning to it. Interestingly, this suggests a way to get rid of an earworm: listen to the whole song from beginning to end. Another trick that seems to work is chewing gum, possibly because it keeps busy the same parts of the brain we use to silently sing along.

What is the main topic of the talk?

AHow musicians write popular songs
BWhy some songs get stuck in our heads and how to stop them
CThe effects of chewing gum on the brain
DWhy people prefer fast music to slow music
Show answer

B. The talk defines earworms, describes what these songs have in common, explains why they repeat and gives two ways to get rid of them. B covers both halves. C is a detail from the last sentence. D distorts a detail: fast tempo is one feature of earworms, not a preference. A is too far from the talk.
