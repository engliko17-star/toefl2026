Factual and Negative Factual

Factual questions are the most common question type in the Reading section. They ask about information the text states directly: a detail, a reason, a date, or the main idea of the whole text. Negative Factual questions turn this around and ask which piece of information is not in the text.

Factual questions

A Factual question checks whether you understood what the author actually said. It may be about:

  • a specific detail: a time, a place, a reason, a requirement, a result;
  • the main idea or purpose of the whole text: What is the email mainly about? What is the main purpose of the notice?
  • information spread over more than one sentence: you may need to put two facts together, as in Example 1 below.

The answer is always in the text, but almost never in the same words. The correct option paraphrases the text: it keeps the meaning and changes the wording.

Typical wording

  • According to the passage, …
  • According to paragraph 2, why …?
  • Which of the following is true about …?
  • What does the author say about …?
  • What is the passage / email mainly about?
  • What is the main purpose of the notice?

Negative Factual questions

In a Negative Factual question, three options are supported by the text. Your job is to find the fourth, which is either not mentioned at all or contradicted by the text. You will recognize these questions by the capitalized word NOT or EXCEPT.

Typical wording

  • According to the passage, which of the following is NOT true of …?
  • All of the following are mentioned in the passage EXCEPT
  • The notice supports all of these statements EXCEPT
Smart Tip Negative Factual questions take longer because you must check all four options against the text. Mark each option in your head as yes, yes, yes, no. The one that is not in the text, or that the text says is false, is your answer. An option that sounds possible but that the text never mentions is still the correct answer.

Strategies for Factual and Negative Factual Questions

Step 1: Find the topic of the question.

Every question names what it is about: the pool, moonlight solar panels, Lena. If it names a paragraph, the answer is in that paragraph. Use the topic word to find the right sentences.

Step 2: Read around the key sentence.

Read the sentence that contains the topic, plus the one before and the one after. Factual answers often depend on a detail in the next sentence.

Step 3: Expect paraphrase, not a copy.

The correct option says the same thing in other words. Be careful with options that copy exact words from the text: they often change the relationship between those words.

Step 4: Check the claim, not only the topic.

Wrong options usually talk about the right topic but say something the author did not say about it. Ask: does the text say this exact thing about the topic?

Step 5: For main idea: separate the main point from details.

Look at the beginning and the end of the text, but be careful: a text can begin or end with a minor detail. The main idea is the one that all the other sentences support.

Step 6: For NOT / EXCEPT: test every option.

Do not stop at the first option that looks wrong. Check all four against the text and choose the one that is not supported.

Common Traps

  • Right topic, wrong claim. The option is about the thing the question asks about, but it changes what the text says about it.
  • Copied words, changed relationship. The option repeats words from the text but reverses a cause and effect, or links two facts that the text does not link.
  • Too narrow or too broad. For main-idea questions, one option covers only one detail, another goes far beyond the text.
  • Real-world knowledge. In Negative Factual questions, the answer may be something that is true in real life but simply not mentioned in the text.
Example 1: a notice (Daily Life)
RECREATION CENTER — SPRING HOURS

Starting April 1:
Mon–Fri: 6:00 a.m.–10:00 p.m.
Sat–Sun: 8:00 a.m.–6:00 p.m.

The pool closes one hour before the building every day.

Towel service is now free for all members. Just show your card at the front desk.

Group classes require sign-up in the campus app. Walk-ins are welcome only if spaces remain.

1. At what time does the pool close on Saturdays?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: A

Explanation

No sentence gives this time directly. You need two facts: the building closes at 6:00 p.m. on weekends, and the pool closes one hour before the building. 6:00 − 1 hour = 5:00 p.m. Factual questions sometimes ask you to combine information from two places like this.

  • Choice B – this is when the building closes, not the pool.
  • Choice C – this is one hour before the weekday closing time.
  • Choice D – this is the weekday closing time of the building.

2. According to the notice, all of the following are true EXCEPT:

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: D

Explanation

This is a Negative Factual question: three options are supported by the notice, and you need the one that is not. The notice says towel service is now free, so D contradicts the text.

  • A is stated: "show your card at the front desk."
  • B is stated: walk-ins are welcome "if spaces remain."
  • C is stated: 6:00 a.m. on weekdays, 8:00 a.m. on weekends.
Example 2: an academic paragraph

Why Cities Are Warmer

Cities are often warmer than the countryside around them, especially at night. One reason is that roads, roofs, and parking lots are made of dark materials that absorb sunlight during the day and slowly release the heat after sunset. Cities also have fewer trees and plants, which cool the air by releasing water through their leaves. In addition, cars, factories, and air conditioners give off heat of their own. Scientists call this effect the "urban heat island," and it can make summer heat waves more dangerous for city residents.

1. What is the paragraph mainly about?

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: B

Explanation

A main idea question is a Factual question about the whole text. The first sentence states the topic (cities are warmer than the countryside) and the rest of the paragraph gives three reasons. B covers all of it.

  • Choice A – air conditioners are one small detail, mentioned only as a source of heat.
  • Choice C – the paragraph says cities have fewer trees, not that trees grow poorly there.
  • Choice D – heat waves appear only in the last sentence; the paragraph gives no advice on protection.

2. According to the paragraph, all of the following add to the warmth of cities EXCEPT

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: D

Explanation

The paragraph names three causes: dark surfaces (A), fewer trees and plants (B), and heat from cars, factories and air conditioners (C). Tall buildings are never mentioned, so D is the answer.

Notice the trap: in real life, buildings that block the wind can make cities warmer. That does not matter. Negative Factual questions ask what this text says, not what is true in the world.
