The Listening Section

The Listening section checks how well you understand spoken English in two kinds of situations: everyday campus life (chatting with a friend, hearing a notice from a campus office) and academic settings (a short talk given by a teacher). You hear each recording only once, and then answer multiple-choice questions about it.

The recordings sound like real speech, not like a textbook. Speakers use contractions and reduced forms (gonna, wanna), they hesitate, restart sentences, drift off topic and interrupt each other politely. You will hear accents from North America, the United Kingdom, Australia and New Zealand.

Every question is multiple choice with one correct answer. There are no "choose two" questions, no tables to fill in and nothing to type.

What You Will Hear: 4 Task Types

The section is built from four types of tasks. They go from the shortest recording (one sentence) to the longest (a short lecture of up to 250 words).

Task type What you hear Length Questions per recording Level
Listen and Choose a Response One question or statement from a person One short sentence 1 A1–B2
Listen to a Conversation A dialogue between two people about everyday or campus topics 35–100 words 2 A2–C1
Listen to an Announcement One person making an announcement in class, at a campus event or on campus radio 40–85 words 2 A2–C1
Listen to an Academic Talk A short lecture or podcast-style talk on history, science, art, business, etc. 175–250 words 4 A2–C2

You never need background knowledge. Everything you need to answer is in the recording, even in the academic talks.

What the Screen Looks Like

For every recording, you see a picture of the speaker (or two speakers in a conversation). There is no text of the recording on the screen. After the audio ends, the question and four answer options appear next to the picture.

How the Two Modules Work

The Listening section is multistage adaptive. It is split into two parts called modules, and the second module depends on how well you did in the first one.

Step by step:

  1. Module 1 (the "router"). Everyone starts with a module of medium difficulty. It contains all four task types, from one-sentence prompts to academic talks.
  2. The test counts your correct answers. Based on how you did, it chooses which second module you get.
  3. Module 2: Upper or Lower. If you did well, you get the Upper module: harder questions and longer recordings. If you did less well, you get the Lower module: easier questions and shorter recordings.
  4. Once Module 2 starts, Module 1 is closed. (In Listening you can't go back even inside a module, so this rule changes nothing in practice.)

Why Module 1 matters so much

  • Your score depends on your path, not only on the number of correct answers. The final score takes into account both how many questions you answered correctly and how difficult your second module was. With the same 30 correct answers, the student who took the Upper module gets a higher score than the student who took the Lower module.
  • The Lower module works like a ceiling. Its questions are easier, so correct answers there are worth less on the final scale. If you aim for a high band (5 or above), plan to reach the Upper module rather than hope to make up for it later.
  • The first 12 or so questions are the easiest to lose by accident. Module 1 opens with a long series of one-sentence prompts. They are short, but each one counts exactly like a question about a lecture. Stay focused from the very first question.

32 questions, but only 20 of them count

You may have noticed that Module 1 has 32 questions, but the tables below show only 20 scored questions in it. The difference is unscored (tryout) questions. The test uses them to check new questions for future tests; they do not affect your score. A Listening module can contain up to 12 of them.

You cannot tell which questions are unscored. They look exactly like the others. So treat every question as if it counts.

Most of this time is spent listening, and you can't speed up a recording. The time you actually control is the few seconds between the end of the audio and your click on Next. Don't spend too long on one question: if you are unsure, eliminate what you can, choose the best remaining option and move on. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a question blank.

6 Question Types

Listen and Choose a Response has its own format: you just pick the best reply. The other three task types (conversations, announcements and academic talks) use the same six types of questions. Once you learn to recognize the type, you know what to listen for.

Question typeWhat it checksTypical wording
Main Idea The overall topic or the speaker's main point What is the conversation mainly about? · What is the main topic of the talk?
Factual A detail that the speaker states directly According to the speaker, … · What does the woman say about …?
Inference What is implied but not said, including what someone will probably do next What does the speaker imply about …? · What will the man probably do next?
Purpose Why the speaker says something or mentions an example Why does the speaker mention …? · Why does the man say, "…"?
Method How the speaker organizes or explains an idea (examples, comparison, steps) How does the speaker explain …? · How does the professor introduce …?
Attitude The speaker's opinion or feelings about something What is the speaker's attitude toward …? · How does the woman feel about …?

How Listening Is Scored

  1. Each correct answer gives 1 point. A wrong or skipped answer gives 0. Unscored questions don't count at all.
  2. Your points from both modules are added up into a raw score from 0 to 35 (20 scored questions in Module 1 + 15 in Module 2).
  3. The raw score is converted into a band score from 1 to 6, in steps of 0.5. The conversion takes into account the difficulty of your path, so it is not a simple percentage.

The Rules to Remember

Each recording plays once

There is no replay button, even for the one-sentence prompts.

Questions go in order

No Back, no Review. After Next, the previous question is gone.

You don't see the questions in advance

The questions appear only after the recording, so listen for everything important.

You may take notes

Notes are not scored. Use them for the longer recordings.

General strategies for the Listening section

  • Take smart notes, not a transcript. Write down the main idea, key details (names, times, places, numbers) and how the points connect: problem → solution, cause → effect, idea A vs idea B. Short words, arrows and symbols work best. For one-sentence prompts, don't write at all; just listen.
  • Always ask two questions while you listen: Who is speaking and where? and Why are they saying this? Almost every task has a question about the topic or the speaker's purpose.
  • Expect paraphrase. The correct answer rarely repeats the speaker's words. An option that uses the exact words from the recording is often a trap.
  • Listen to tone and signal phrases. Intonation shows whether someone agrees, is surprised or is joking. Phrases like "Let's focus on…", "For example…", "On the other hand…" show how a talk is organized.
  • Let go of a missed question. You can't go back, so worrying about the previous answer only makes you miss the next recording.
  • Train with real, natural English. Podcasts, YouTube interviews, campus vlogs and university lectures with different accents are the best preparation, especially for the informal language in conversations and short prompts.
