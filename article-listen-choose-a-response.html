Listen and Choose a Response

In this task, a person says one short sentence to you: a question, a request, an offer or just a comment. You hear it once, and the sentence is never shown on the screen. Then you read four written replies and choose the one that would naturally come next in the conversation.

It is the simplest-looking task in the section, and it opens the Listening section, so it sets the tone for everything after it. It checks more than vocabulary: you need to understand what the speaker wants and which reply is socially right in that situation.

What you hearOne question or statement from one speaker
What you seeA photo of the speaker and 4 written replies. No text of what was said.
Length of the audioOne sentence, usually 4–15 words
Questions per audio1
How many in the sectionAbout 15–19 questions: roughly 12 at the start of Module 1 and 3–7 at the start of Module 2
Scored questions8 in Module 1, then 7 (Lower) or 3 (Upper) in Module 2
LevelA1–B2
TopicsEveryday campus and social life: classes, friends, food, schedules, services

What the Screen Looks Like

  • The instruction at the top is always the same: Choose the best response.
  • The audio starts automatically. The options are on the screen, so you can glance at them before the audio begins, but they won't tell you what the speaker will say.
  • A reply can be a full sentence, a question or just a short phrase, for example "Sure, go ahead." or "Since when?"
  • There is exactly one correct reply. The other three are designed to sound possible if you catch only part of the sentence.

What This Task Tests

Everyday vocabulary and set phrases

Would you mind…, How come…, I'm afraid…, Go ahead, Not at all, Count me in

Question grammar

Hearing the difference between Who / When / Where / Why / How questions, tag questions and negative questions

Sounds, stress and intonation

Telling apart similar words (walk / work, fifteen / fifty) and hearing surprise, doubt or a complaint in the voice

Implied meaning and social rules

Understanding what the speaker really wants and which reply is polite and natural in that situation

Step 1: What Is the Speaker Doing?

The key to this task is to recognize the function of the sentence you hear. The same words can ask for information, ask for help or complain. Each function expects a different kind of reply.

FunctionYou hearA natural reply
Wh- questionWhere are you meeting your group?In the lounge on the third floor.
Yes/no questionIs the cafeteria open on Sundays?Only for brunch, I think.
Negative questionDidn't you already take that course?No, I dropped it last spring.
Tag questionThe exam's on Thursday, isn't it?Actually, it got moved to Friday.
RequestCould you send me the slides from today?Sure, I'll email them tonight.
Asking permissionDo you mind if I sit here?Not at all. Go ahead.
OfferWant me to carry some of those boxes?That would be great, thanks.
Suggestion / invitationWhy don't we grab a coffee after class?Sounds good. I could use one.
Statement / newsThe library's closing early today.Then I'd better return these books now.
Complaint / feelingI can't believe the bus is late again.I know. It's been late all week.

The form can hide the function

"Why don't we…?" looks like a Why question, but it is a suggestion. So a reply starting with "Because…" is wrong. In the same way, "Would you mind…?" is a request, and the polite yes to it is "Not at all" or "No problem". And "Do you know where…?" is not really a yes/no question: the speaker wants the place, not a "Yes, I do."

Step 2: Expect Indirect Answers

In real conversations, people often don't answer the question directly. They give a reason, an excuse or new information, and the listener understands the answer from it. The test uses this a lot, and the correct reply is often the indirect one.

Are you going to the career fair tomorrow?

I've got a lab until five. → means "probably not"

When does the next shuttle leave?

Let me check the schedule. → doesn't know yet, but will find out

Can I borrow your calculator for the quiz?

Sorry, I need it myself. → a polite "no" is still a correct reply

Notice the last example: the correct reply doesn't have to agree. What matters is that it fits what the speaker said. A refusal, a question back ("Which one do you need?") or "I'm not sure" can all be correct.

Step 3: Know the Traps

The three wrong options are built in predictable ways. Once you know them, you will spot them quickly. All examples below answer the same prompt:

"When are you handing in your lab report?"

TrapWrong replyWhy it's wrong
Same or similar wordsThe lab is in the science building.Repeats lab but talks about a place, not a time. Hearing a word from the prompt does not make a reply correct.
Sound-alike wordsI reported it to the manager.Reported sounds like report but means something else.
Answers a different question wordTo Professor Diaz's office.Answers Where?, not When?
Wrong time or tenseI handed it in last week.The speaker assumes it is still to be done. If it were already handed in, a natural reply would correct them ("Actually, I already did"), not just state a fact.
Wrong rolesThanks, I'll let you know.Reacts as if someone offered help. Watch who is asking and who is offering.
CorrectProbably on Friday, after I finish the graphs.Answers when and fits the situation.

Strategies for Listen and Choose a Response

  • Catch the first words. The beginning of the sentence (Where…, Could you…, Why don't we…, Didn't you…) tells you the function. If you miss everything else, the first words alone often let you eliminate two options.
  • Name the function in your head. Question? Request? Offer? Complaint? Then look for the reply that does the right job: answers, agrees or refuses, accepts or declines, sympathizes.
  • Listen to the voice. Rising or surprised intonation, a sigh, an annoyed tone can change the meaning. "You're leaving already?" with surprise is not a simple yes/no question.
  • Be suspicious of repeated words. An option that repeats a word from the prompt, or a word that sounds like it, is very often a trap.
  • Read each option as the second line of a dialogue. Say the prompt and the reply together in your head. Does it sound like two real people talking? The correct pair sounds natural; the wrong ones sound strange or off-topic.
  • Don't take notes and don't dwell. This task is fast. Answer, click Next and get ready: the next prompt starts right away.
Example

You hear (a man):

"Aren't you coming to the review session tonight?"

Choose the best response.

AI'm afraid I've got a shift at the café.
BYes, the session was really useful.
CNo, I didn't review the chapter.
DIt's in the room next to the café.
Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: A

Explanation

"Aren't you coming…?" is a negative question. The man expected his friend to come and is a little surprised that it seems she isn't. The session is tonight, so it is still in the future. Choice A answers indirectly: she has to work, so she can't come. The polite "I'm afraid" shows she is refusing.

  • Choice B uses the past (was), but the session hasn't happened yet.
  • Choice C repeats the sound of review, but talks about reviewing a chapter, not about coming to the session.
  • Choice D tells where the session is, which answers Where?, a question nobody asked.

Question 1 of 12

Transcript: "Do you know if the bookstore is still open?"

Choose the best response.

AIt closes at six on weekdays.
BI bought two books there yesterday.
CNo, I don't know him very well.
DYes, it's a really big store.
Show answer

A. "Do you know if…?" is a polite way to ask for information, so the speaker wants to know whether the store is open. A gives the closing time, which answers the question indirectly. D says "yes" but talks about size. B repeats the topic but not the question. C answers "Do you know him?".

Question 2 of 12

ABecause the lab was cancelled.
BGood idea. I'm starving.
CI ate there last week.
DThe lab is on the second floor.
Show answer

B. "Why don't we…?" is a suggestion, not a real why-question. B accepts it naturally. A is the classic trap: it answers "Why?" with "Because…". C and D reuse words (eat, lab) without responding to the suggestion.

Review

1. Function

Question, request, offer, suggestion or comment? The first words usually tell you.

2. Fit

The right reply does the right job, even indirectly: an excuse, a refusal or a question back can be correct.

3. Traps

Repeated or sound-alike words, the wrong question word, the wrong tense, swapped roles.
