The Reading Section: How It Works

Reading is the first section of the TOEFL iBT® test. In about 30 minutes you answer around 50 questions about three kinds of texts: academic paragraphs with missing letters, short everyday texts such as emails and notices, and short academic passages. This guide explains how the section is built, how the two modules work, what you see on the screen, and how your answers become a score on the 1–6 scale.

The three task types

Every question in the section belongs to one of three tasks. Each task measures a different side of reading, and each has its own length and number of questions.

Notice the level ranges. Complete the Words and academic passages do not go down to A1–A2: the lowest levels are measured mainly through the Daily Life texts. Academic passages are the only task that reaches C2.

Two modules: how the adaptive format works

Reading is a multistage adaptive section. Everyone starts with the same kind of first module, called the router. Its questions are of medium difficulty, around CEFR B1–B2. How well you do there decides which second module you get: a lower one or an upper one. Adaptation happens once, between the modules, not after every question.

Look at what each second module contains. The lower module has Daily Life texts but no academic passages. The upper module has academic passages but no Daily Life texts. Both include one Complete the Words paragraph. So across the whole section, the two possible paths look like this:

Why Module 1 has 35 questions but only 20 count

Module 1 has about 35 questions, but only 20 of them count. The other 15 or so are tryout questions: ETS uses them to test new material for future tests. They look exactly like the scored ones, and nothing on the screen tells them apart.

In the two full practice tests in the Official Guide, the tryout block was one Complete the Words paragraph and one academic passage, but they sat in different places: in Practice Test 1 it was the second paragraph and the first passage, in Practice Test 2 the first paragraph and the second passage. You cannot know which questions are tryouts, so treat every question as if it counts.

In both full practice tests in the Official Guide, Module 1 went in this order: two Complete the Words paragraphs, a Daily Life text with 2 questions, a Daily Life text with 3 questions, and two academic passages with 5 questions each (10 + 10 + 2 + 3 + 5 + 5 = 35). Module 2 began with one Complete the Words paragraph followed by the texts. The real test may vary, but this is the layout to expect.

Smart Tip Do not try to work out during the test which module you are in. It changes nothing about how you should answer, and it costs time and focus. What matters is accuracy, especially in Module 1, because Module 1 decides your path.

Timing

Each module has its own clock: up to 21 minutes for Module 1 and up to 9 minutes for Module 2, about 30 minutes in total. The time for directions is not included. ETS's specifications give the router as 18–21 minutes, and the Official Guide's practice tests allow about 20 minutes for Module 1, so plan on roughly 20.

Module 1 is long: about 35 questions, including two academic passages. Without a plan, many students spend too long on the first texts and rush the last passage. This is how we suggest dividing the time:

A few rules matter for strategy:

  • You can move back and forth freely inside a module. Once you start Module 2, you cannot return to Module 1.
  • The text stays on the screen while you answer its questions. You never need to answer from memory.
  • Each Daily Life or academic question appears on its own screen, with the text on the left and the question on the right.
  • You may take notes during the whole test; the notes are destroyed at the end. For most Reading questions, notes are not necessary because the text is in front of you.

The eight question types

Daily Life texts and academic passages are followed by multiple-choice questions of eight types. ETS divides them into two groups: questions about what the text says (basic comprehension) and questions about what it implies, why something is there, and how ideas connect (inferential comprehension). Each type has its own guide:

How the section is scored

  • Every scored question is worth 1 point, whatever its type or difficulty. In Complete the Words, each word is a separate question. Your raw score is 0–35.
  • There is no penalty for wrong answers. A blank answer and a wrong answer both get 0, so never leave a question empty.
  • The raw score is converted to the 1–6 scale through a statistical process called equating. It takes into account which path you took: the same number of correct answers is worth more on the harder upper path.
  • The conversion is not a percentage and not a simple formula. Each test version has its own conversion table, and ETS does not publish the tables for the real test.
  • Scores are reported in half points (1, 1.5, 2 … 6). Your Reading and Listening scores appear on the screen as unofficial scores when you finish the test; official scores arrive within 72 hours.

A reference point: the practice-test table

For its two linear practice tests, the Official Guide gives a conversion table. Some points from it: 35 correct = 6; 32–33 = 5.5; 28–31 = 5; 25–27 = 4.5; 18–24 = 4; 12–17 = 3.5; 8–11 = 3.

Use it only as a rough guide. The real test is adaptive, so the table for your test depends on your path and the test version.

ETS reports a reliability of 0.86 for the Reading section and a standard error of measurement of 0.37 points. In practice, your score could differ by about half a point if you took a different version of the test on another day.

General strategies

Strategies for the Whole Section

Step 1: Answer everything.

Wrong answers cost nothing. If you are unsure, remove the options that are clearly wrong and choose the best of the rest.

Step 2: Do not get stuck.

If a question takes too long, choose your best guess, note the question number, and come back through Review if time allows.

Step 3: Protect Module 1.

Your Module 1 score decides whether you reach the upper module. Accuracy there is worth more than speed in the last minutes.

Step 4: Read for the job, not for every word.

You do not need to understand every word of a passage to answer its questions. Get the general idea first, then read closely only the parts a question asks about.

Step 5: Expect paraphrase.

Correct answers rarely copy the text word for word. Options that repeat the text's exact words are often traps.

Step 6: Know the task types before test day.

The directions and screens are always the same. If you already know them, you will not lose time working out what to do.

Outside the test, the most effective preparation is regular reading: textbook-style articles on many subjects, but also emails, websites, ads and social media posts in English. Keep a vocabulary list organized by topic and pay attention to how texts are organized: cause and effect, comparison, problem and solution.
