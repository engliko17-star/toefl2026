Insert Text

In an Insert Text question you get one extra sentence that is not in the passage, and four black squares in the text that mark possible places for it. Only one place works. On the test you click a square, and the sentence appears in the passage at that point, so you can read the result before you decide.

How it works on the test

The passage is on the left with four black squares, usually within one paragraph. The new sentence is on the right. Clicking a square places the sentence in that position in the passage. You can click another square to move it. In printed practice material the squares are replaced by letters [A]–[D].

In ETS's practice material this type was frequent: 5 of the 6 academic passages in the two full practice tests ended with an Insert Text question.

What job does the new sentence do?

The new sentence usually does one of two kinds of job. Knowing which one it does tells you what must be around it.

KindThe new sentence…So it must come…
Adds contentgives a cause or result of something just mentionedright after that thing
gives an example or a statistic for a claimright after the claim
adds a limitation or exception (However, not all …)after the claim it limits
Organizes the textintroduces a topic that is about to be discussedbefore that discussion

links two sentences, showing how they are relatedbetween those two sentences

Language clues

Most of the time, the new sentence contains words that only work in one position. These "hooks" are your fastest route to the answer:

Smart Tip A repeated or similar word is a clue, but not proof. A sentence about the experiment does not always go right after the word experiment. Always check that the sentence also fits with the sentence that comes after it.

Strategies for Insert Text Questions

Step 1: Read the new sentence carefully.

Find its hooks: a signal word, a pronoun or this/these, a repeated key word, also or another, an article like the that suggests the thing was mentioned before.

Step 2: Ask what each hook needs.

As a result needs a cause before it. This method needs a method before it. Another reason needs a first reason. For example needs a general statement.

Step 3: Check the content.

Does the sentence add a detail, an example, a result, or does it introduce or conclude? Match it to the part of the paragraph that it develops.

Step 4: Check both sides.

The sentence must fit with the sentence before it and not break the sentence after it. Watch for pronouns in the next sentence that would suddenly point to the wrong thing.

Step 5: Test-insert.

On the test, click each plausible square and read the paragraph with the sentence in place. If it sounds natural and logical in one place only, that is your answer.

Common Traps

  • Fits before, breaks after. The sentence connects well to the previous sentence but makes the next one confusing or illogical.
  • Key-word match only. The square comes right after a word that appears in the new sentence, but the logic does not work.
  • Interrupting a chain. Putting the sentence between a sentence and the pronoun that refers back to it.
  • Splitting a list. Placing a sentence in the middle of first … second … finally.
Example 1: a sentence that adds a result

Autumn Leaves

Why do the leaves of many trees change color in autumn?

During spring and summer, leaves are green because they contain large amounts of chlorophyll, the pigment plants use to capture energy from sunlight. [A] Leaves also contain yellow and orange pigments called carotenoids, but these are normally hidden by the green. [B] In autumn, as the days grow shorter and cooler, trees stop producing chlorophyll, and the green color gradually breaks down. [C] Some trees, such as maples, also produce new red pigments at this time of year. [D]

1. Where would the following sentence best fit?
As a result, the yellow and orange colors that were there all along finally become visible.

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: C

Explanation

The new sentence has three hooks. As a result needs a cause just before it. Were there all along needs the yellow and orange colors to have been mentioned already. Finally become visible needs something that stops hiding them. All three are satisfied only at [C]: the carotenoids were introduced earlier, and the sentence before [C] says the green chlorophyll breaks down. When the green disappears, the hidden colors show.

  • Choice A – the yellow and orange colors have not been mentioned yet, so "there all along" has nothing to refer to.
  • Choice B – the previous sentence says the colors are hidden; "as a result … become visible" would contradict it.
  • Choice D – new red pigments do not cause yellow and orange to appear; the cause is the loss of green.
Example 2: a sentence that introduces the topic

Passage excerpt

[A] First, it is cheap to grow, since many species are ready to harvest after only a few years. [B] Second, it is surprisingly strong for its weight, which makes it useful for scaffolding and frames. [C] Finally, because it absorbs carbon dioxide as it grows, it may help reduce the environmental cost of construction. [D]

1. Where would the following sentence best fit?
Bamboo has several advantages as a building material.

Answer + Explanation

Correct Answer: A

Explanation

This sentence plays a structural role: it introduces the topic. The paragraph lists advantages with First … Second … Finally, and a list like that needs an introduction that tells the reader what is being listed. Only [A] puts the sentence before the list.

  • Choice B – it would split the list after the first point.
  • Choice C – it would split the list after the second point.
  • Choice D – an introduction cannot come after the list is finished.
